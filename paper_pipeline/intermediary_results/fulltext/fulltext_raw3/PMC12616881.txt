ABSTRACT
This observational study investigated the potential influence of hormonal contraceptive (HC) use on selected hormonal biomarkers (estradiol, total testosterone, IGF‐1, cortisol, triiodothyronine, and leptin) that respond to diet and physical training–induced low energy availability (LEA). Thirty‐six female physique athletes—combined HC users (
n
= 11), low‐dose progestin‐only HC users (
n
= 11), and naturally menstruating females (
n
= 14)—prepared for a physique competition. All participants voluntarily restricted energy intake while simultaneously participating in resistance training to maintain lean mass and aerobic training to increase energy expenditure. Measurements were completed over approximately 46 weeks as follows: before commencing the pre‐competition period, including dietary restriction and physical training (pre); after ∼23 weeks of dietary restriction and physical training (post); and after ∼23 weeks of recovery (recovery). Hormones were analyzed from fasting blood samples, whereas body mass, fat mass, fat‐free mass, and body fat percentage were measured using bioimpedance. Our main findings established that after ∼23 weeks of LEA, concentrations of estradiol and total testosterone remained stable in the combined HC users but decreased significantly in progestin‐only HC users and naturally menstruating females. Serum IGF‐1, triiodothyronine, and leptin decreased comparably from pre to post in all groups, whereas serum cortisol concentrations remained statistically unchanged throughout the investigation. These results indicate that the exogenous hormones in combined HCs users may maintain already suppressed hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis function in the presence of LEA, whereas LEA‐induced changes in IGF‐1, triiodothyronine, and leptin may be comparable in combined and progestin‐only HC users and naturally menstruating females.

Highlights
Combined hormonal contraceptive users maintain already suppressed hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis function in the presence of low energy availability (maintained estradiol and total testosterone concentrations), but hormonal contraceptive users and naturally menstruating females experience comparable, potentially negative, and endocrine consequences of low energy availability including significant decreases in concentrations of IGF‐1, triiodothyronine, and leptin.
Hormonal contraceptive users and physicians prescribing hormonal contraceptives should be made aware that diet and physical training–induced low energy availability might negatively affect endocrine health.
The synergistic effects of combined hormonal contraceptive use and diet and physical training–induced low energy availability require attention in both applied and clinical research.

Introduction
Hormonal contraceptive (HC) use is relatively common in physically active and athletic populations. Roughly 50% of elite female athletes use HCs, where combined oral HCs are currently the most common (up to 70% of HC users) (Martin et al.
2018
) and the use of hormonal (progestin‐only) intrauterine devices (IUDs) is increasing (Ekenros et al.
2022
) although its prevalence is country‐dependent (Lindh et al.
2017
; United Nations
2019
). Combined HCs are composed of an estrogen (synthetic ethinyl estradiol) and a progestin (synthetic progesterone), where progestin‐only HCs contain only progestin. Combined HCs suppress hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian (HPO) function, resulting in decreased pituitary gonadotropin secretion and lower concentrations of endogenous female sex steroids such as estradiol (E2) and progesterone (Rivera et al.
1999
). Progestin‐only HCs may suppress HPO axis function in higher exogenous doses (Teal and Edelman
2021
) whereas lower exogenous doses such as those delivered by minipills and hormonal IUDs do not suppress HPO axis function or do so inconsistently (Spencer et al.
2009
; Grimes et al.
2013
). As such, the endogenous sex steroid profile in low‐dose progestin‐only users may closely mimic that of naturally menstruating/eumenorrheic individuals (Spencer et al.
2009
; Grimes et al.
2013
).
While the primary purpose of combined HCs is to suppress female sex steroid production, combined HCs also tend to decrease circulating concentrations of total and free testosterone with a concomitant increase in sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG) concentrations (Zimmerman et al.
2014
). Combined HCs also tend to influence the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis resulting in higher total cortisol concentrations in combined HC users than nonusers (Hertel et al.
2017
; Masama et al.
2022
) although this observation is inconsistent (Kirschbaum et al.
1996
; Larsen et al.
2020
). An increase in growth hormone (GH) concentrations via estrogen's effect on the pituitary gland, concomitant with a decrease in insulin‐like growth factor 1 (IGF‐1), may be also observed in combined HC users (Southmayd and De Souza
2017
; Birzniece and Ho
2021
). The decrease in IGF‐1 may be due to estrogen's direct inhibitory effect on IGF‐1 synthesis in the liver or altered GH signaling; however, there does not appear to be significant differences in GH or IGF‐1 concentrations between naturally menstruating females and combined HC users (Massa et al.
1993
; Riechman and Lee
2022
; Özcan et al.
2024
). Combined HCs, such as those containing, for example, dienogest and levonorgestrel, may increase thyroid hormones [total triiodothyronine (T3) and thyroxine (T4)] due to an increase in binding to serum globulins, but the free fractions of these hormones appear to be only slightly changed or unchanged (Torre et al.
2020
). The systemic effects of progestin‐only HCs have not been as widely investigated, although it has been suggested that progestin‐only HCs only have a minor effect on systemic metabolism and inflammation (Wang et al.
2016
). Indeed, progestin‐only pills containing desogestrel or levonorgestrel do not appear to significantly affect thyroid function (Kivelä et al.
2001
).
Low energy availability (LEA), an energy availability (EA) of < 30 kcal·kg
−1
FFM·day
−1
], refers to a mismatch between dietary energy intakes (EI) to cover the energy cost of exercise (exercise energy expenditure, EEE), which results in suboptimal energy for physiological processes in the body, including those related to the maintenance of health and supporting training adaptations in athletes (Loucks et al.
2011
). The equation EA = (EI−EEE)/FFM (where FFM = fat‐free mass) is often used to estimate EA, although protocols for evaluating EI, EEE, and FFM have not universally been agreed upon thus leaving some room for error (Burke et al.
2018
). The effects of LEA, which may be induced by, for example, diet and physical training, on endocrine function have been reported in several publications (Elliott‐Sale et al.
2018
; Ackerman and Misra
2018
; Dipla et al.
2020
; Areta et al.
2020
; Ihalainen et al.
2024
). For example, LEA has been reported to decrease gonadotropin‐releasing hormone resulting in downstream suppression of luteinizing hormone, estrogen, and testosterone whereas a decrease in thyroid hormones (e.g., T3), leptin, IGF‐1, and insulin and an increase in cortisol, growth hormone, and ghrelin have been reported (Elliott‐Sale et al.
2018
; Ackerman and Misra
2018
; Dipla et al.
2020
; Areta et al.
2020
; Ihalainen et al.
2024
). Much of this research has focused on menstrual dysfunction and bone health, as well as the identification of surrogate markers of LEA in the context of the female athlete triad (Yeager et al.
1993
; Otis et al.
1997
) or relative energy deficiency in sport (REDs) (Mountjoy et al.
2014
,
2018
,
2023
) models, whereas the possible influence of physical and psychological stressors on hormonal function are less discussed (Facchinetti et al.
1993
; Nappi and Facchinetti
2003
). To our knowledge, references to HC use in research on LEA in athletes (both in the triad and REDs models) are generally limited to reminders that (combined) HC provides exogenous hormones that suppress sex hormone concentrations that are likely to mask menstrual dysfunction, such as the ovulatory disorders (Munro et al.
2022
), associated with LEA. At present, we are unaware of any literature that specifically discusses the possible influence of progestin‐only HC use on hormonal markers associated with LEA. Where exogenous sex steroids in combined HCs may continue to regulate the HPO axis when faced with LEA and other stressors, how other hormonal axes might respond to LEA and other stressors remains to be elucidated.
Given the systemic hormonal changes induced by HCs as well as LEA (and other stressors), it is fair to hypothesize that the exogenous hormones in HCs may have implications for biomarkers used for monitoring athlete health. As such, the purpose of this observational study was to examine data (secondary analysis) from two physique athlete studies carried out at the University of Jyväskylä that was stratified into groups based on HC use (combined HC and progestin‐only HC) and nonuse that is, naturally menstruating females, in order to investigate the possible differences in responses between these groups in terms of changes in selected hormones after planned intentional exposure to LEA induced by diet and physical training. We hypothesized that combined HCs would maintain (already suppressed) concentrations of hormones associated directly with the HPO axis (estradiol and testosterone) during LEA but that combined HC and progestin‐only HC users would experience comparable endocrine changes to participants with natural cycles in hormones not directly regulated by the HPO axis, specifically IGF‐1, cortisol, triiodothyronine, and leptin.

Methods
The data presented herein were extracted from two physique athlete research studies carried out at the University of Jyväskylä in 2015–2016 and 2019–2020 [data published elsewhere (Hulmi et al.
2017
; Sarin, Gudelj, et al.
2019
, Sarin, Lee, et al.
2019
; Sarin et al.
2021
; Isola et al.
2023
,
2024
)]. Physique athletes are a unique group to research as these athletes voluntarily conduct prolonged ∼3–5‐month periods of energy restriction concurrent to higher volumes of strength and endurance training resulting in LEA.
Participants were recruited by advertisements on the University web page, the governing body for physique sport web page (Finland), and social media advertisements. Recruited participants were physique athletes preparing to participate in a physique competition. Participants were excluded if they were diagnosed with chronic diseases, reported using prescribed medications (except for HCs) or any substances or methods prohibited by the World Antidoping Agency (WADA), competing in junior (below 19 years of age) or master (over 40 years of age) categories, or competing in a nondrug‐tested competition category. Additional inclusion criteria consisted of a competition background in physique sports, including body fitness, bikini fitness, wellness fitness, and women's physique, and no competitions in the 6 months preceding the present data collection.
Participants were given comprehensive explanations regarding the study design and protocols as well as possible risks and benefits of study participation. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before participation in the study. Upon commencement of the study, participants were given identification numbers, and the research group was blinded to their diet status. The first study obtained ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the University of Jyväskylä (3/2015) and the second study from the Ethics Committee of the Central Finland Health Care District (19U/2018), Finland and was registered at
ClinicalTrials.gov
ID: NCT04392752. Both studies were conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki.
This secondary analysis included data from 36 participants. Participants were amateur physique athletes aiming to lose fat while maintaining muscle mass through concurrent dieting, resistance training, and aerobic training (Hulmi et al.
2017
; Isola et al.
2023
). The diet herein induced voluntary LEA with planned recovery, thus providing circumstances from which to extrapolate data. Physique athletes are an excellent study population because their sports, judged on aesthetic appearance, necessitate intense weight loss through high volumes of aerobic exercise and resistance training combined with low energy intake, making them ideal participants for assessing physiological changes associated with weight loss in normal weight individuals (Sarin, Lee, et al.
2019
). For this study, groups were stratified by hormonal contraceptive use, as presented in Table
1
. Combined hormonal contraceptive users used formulations containing ethinyl estradiol and either a first (
n

n

n

n

3
Study Design
We did not provide a specific intervention as this was an observational study. All groups followed their preferred diet and physical training regimen (including both endurance and resistance training activities) and were invited to the laboratory for testing sessions over an ∼46‐week period, including competition preparation (diet and physical training for ∼23 weeks) and recovery (∼23 weeks) periods (Hulmi et al.
2017
; Isola et al.
2023
).
Blood samples and body composition were measured at our laboratory between 07.00 and 09.00 a.m. after at least 8 hours of fasting and an instruction to sleep for at least 8 hours. Participants were asked to abstain from alcohol and caffeine for 12 h and from training for 24 h. Furthermore, participants were advised to avoid physical activity, such as walking, jogging, and cycling, on the morning of scheduled measurements. All measurements were conducted at the same time of day (± 1 h).
3.1
Training
Resistance and aerobic training were completed according to individual training programs. Participants were asked to provide their training diaries throughout the study. If participants changed their training program, they were asked to report/record these changes. Training volumes were calculated from the training diaries (total sets per week per muscle group for resistance training and total minutes per week for aerobic training). As described by Hulmi et al.
2017
, resistance training was mainly completed using split routines, meaning that participants trained their upper or lower body on separate days or focused on single muscle groups during each training session, which is a common practice in physique athletes (Hackett et al.
2013
). The main muscle groups trained in these split routines included the thighs, hamstrings, buttocks, chest, shoulders, arms, upper and lower back, calves, and abdominals where resistance training was performed ∼5x ∙ week
−1
throughout the study. Endurance training included either high‐intensity interval training (HIT) using mostly bicycles and cross‐trainers or a combination of HIT and steady‐state low/medium intensity aerobics (mainly walking/running or with cross‐trainer), which was performed ∼4x ∙ week
−1
with an increase in frequency as the competition approached and a decrease in frequency to ∼2x ∙ week
−1
after the competition. For detailed information about training protocols, see Hulmi et al. (
2017
) and Isola et al. (
2023
). Measurements were completed before commencing the diet and the physical training phase (pre), one week before (Hulmi et al.
2017
) or the next day (Isola et al.
2023
) after the competition (post) and after the 23‐week recovery period (recovery). The majority of participants were measured the morning after the competition: 7/11 combined HC users, 10/11 progestin‐only HC users, and 10/14 naturally menstruating females. Additional papers from this project are published elsewhere (Hulmi et al.
2017
; Sarin, Gudelj, et al.
2019
, Sarin, Lee, et al.
2019
,
2021
; Isola et al.
2023
).
3.2
Nutrition Intake and Supplementation
Daily energy and macronutrient intake and dietary supplementation guidance were provided to the athletes by their individual coaches throughout the study. To estimate energy intake, participants provided nutrition logs throughout the study period. If participants made changes to their diet, they were asked to report/record these changes. Four‐day food diaries (3 weekdays and 1 weekend day) were collected at time points corresponding to pre, post, and recovery. Food diary analysis was completed using nutrient analysis software (Aivodiet, Flow‐team Oy, Oulu, Finland). EA was calculated by subtracting exercise energy expenditure from energy intake and expressing the results relative to fat‐free mass.
3.3
Body Composition
Body composition was assessed after overnight fasting using a multi‐frequency bioelectrical impedance analyzer (InBody 720; Biospace, Seoul, Korea) with participants wearing only underwear. Participants were instructed to go to the bathroom prior to measurements and were instructed to consume no more than one glass (2 dL) of water after waking.
3.4
Blood Samples
Venous blood samples were collected from the antecubital vein into serum tubes (Venosafe; Terumo Medical Co., Leuven, Hanau, Belgium) using standard laboratory procedures. Samples were stored at room temperature for 30 min after which they were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min (Megadure 1.0 R Heraeus; DJB Lab Care Germany) to separate serum. Estradiol (cross‐reactivity for ethinyl estradiol was 1.4%), total testosterone (tT), insulin‐like growth factor 1 (IGF‐1), cortisol, free triiodothyronine (T3), and leptin were analyzed from serum using chemical luminescence techniques (Immulite 2000; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany).
3.5
Statistical Analysis
A repeated measures two‐way ANOVA with the between factor being groups (combined HC, progestin‐only HC, and naturally menstruating) and the within factor measurement times (pre, post, and recovery) was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Mauchly's test of sphericity was used to assess the assumption of sphericity. When this assumption was violated, Greenhouse–Geisser adjustments were applied. Post hoc tests for interactions were performed with the Bonferroni adjustments. When a significant main effect for time was observed, a series of exploratory paired sample one‐sided
t
‐tests were conducted to determine where significance was located. Of particular interest were possible changes in hormone concentrations between pre‐ and post‐measurements. Statistical significance was set at
p
≤ 0.05. Figures were made with GraphPad Prism and Microsoft Excel (Version 2404).

6
Discussion
At baseline prior to commencing diet and physical training–induced LEA (pre) concentrations of E2, tT, T3, cortisol, and leptin were comparable between groups but combined HC users had significantly lower concentrations of IGF‐1 than naturally menstruating females. Our main findings established that after ∼23 weeks of voluntarily induced LEA, concentrations of E2 and tT remained stable in combined HC users, likely a result of exogenous hormone consumption, but decreased significantly in progestin‐only HC users and naturally menstruating females. Importantly, serum IGF‐1, triiodothyronine, and leptin decreased comparably in all groups, whereas serum cortisol concentrations remained statistically unchanged throughout the investigation. These observations are in line with our hypothesis that combined HC users would maintain (already suppressed) concentrations of hormones associated directly with the HPO axis (E2 and tT) but that combined HC and progestin‐only HC users would otherwise experience comparable endocrine changes to naturally menstruating participants in hormones not directly regulated by the HPO axis (IGF‐1, cortisol, T3, and leptin). Furthermore, these observations suggest that the endocrine system [including the HPA axis, hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid (HPT) axis, GH‐IGF‐1 axis, and adipose tissue] might respond to the combined stressors of diet and exercise training in a comparable manner irrespective of HC use. As expected, body mass, fat mass, fat‐free mass, and body fat % decreased significantly concurrent to decreased EA in combination with physical training in all groups. Changes in hormones and body composition during recovery from decreased EA in combination with training are difficult to evaluate because progestin‐only users and naturally menstruating females maintained lower EA (< 30 kcal · kg FFM
−1
· day
−1
) during the recovery period, whereas the combined HC group had higher EA (> 40 kcal · kg FFM
−1
· day
−1
).
Low energy availability (Mountjoy et al.
2023
) and other physical and psychological stressors (Facchinetti et al.
1993
; Nappi and Facchinetti
2003
) are known to induce several hormonal changes, including a decrease in gonadotropin‐releasing hormone at the hypothalamus, which results in downstream suppression of luteinizing hormone at the anterior pituitary and estrogen and testosterone at the ovary. In addition, a decrease in thyroid hormones (e.g., T3), leptin, IGF‐1, and insulin as well as an increase in cortisol, growth hormone, and ghrelin have been observed concomitant to LEA (Elliott‐Sale et al.
2018
; Ackerman and Misra
2018
; Dipla et al.
2020
; Areta et al.
2020
; Ihalainen et al.
2024
). Regrettably, it is unclear how these hormones might be influenced by HC use. Heikura et al. (
2018
) suggested that signs of chronic problematic LEA other than menstrual dysfunction, such as low bone mineral density, an increase in bone‐related injuries, and decreased metabolic hormone concentrations, could be used to detect LEA in athletes using HC (Heikura et al.
2018
). However, research regarding the endocrine effects of LEA and other stressors on hormone concentrations in HC users is scarce. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the possible influence of hormonal contraceptives on biomarkers commonly investigated in LEA, and we acknowledge that further research is needed to confirm these findings.
In our study, E2 and tT concentrations remained stable in combined HC users from pre to post, whereas these decreased significantly in progestin‐only HC users and naturally menstruating females. This suggests that combined HCs may have maintained E2 and tT concentrations in the presence of diet and physical training–induced LEA. Importantly, concentrations of E2 in the combined HC group at pre were already low, as combined HC use suppresses HPO axis function. While E2 is responsible for regulating reproductive function in menstruating females (Wierman
2007
), it also has several nonreproductive roles in the body, including the promotion of bone health (Southmayd et al.
2017
), muscle mass and strength (Enns and Tiidus
2010
; Hansen
2018
; Chidi‐Ogbolu and Baar
2019
), metabolism (Hackney
1999
), and cardiovascular function (Ramesh et al.
2019
). These roles are of importance to all females of reproductive age, especially athletes. While combined HCs often decrease basal levels of both free testosterone and tT (Zimmerman et al.
2014
) the present combined HC use may have helped to maintain baseline concentrations of tT in the presence of LEA as only 4/11 formulas used were clearly anti‐androgenic. An increase in SHBG concentrations commonly reported in the literature may have also contributed to this result (Zimmerman et al.
2014
), although it is also possible that exogenous progestin from combined HCs may have also contributed, as progesterone is an upstream precursor for the biosynthesis of T. Furthermore, maintained E2 may be the result of maintained tT as
T
is converted into E2 via peripheral aromatization. Lower or decreased concentrations of
T
have been reported in females with LEA and training‐related menstrual dysfunction (
Rickenlund et al. 2 004
; Bermon et al.
2014
) although higher levels of
T
have also been observed (without characteristics of hyperandrogenism/polycystic ovary syndrome) (Łagowska and Kapczuk
2016
). These findings may be of practical relevance, particularly for progestin‐only HC users, as a decrease in E2, in particular, is a common consequence of LEA in naturally menstruating females that is associated with endocrine and menstrual dysfunction (e.g., ovulatory disorders (Munro et al.
2022
)) as well as decreased performance and a myriad of health issues identified in the triad and REDs models (Yeager et al.
1993
; Otis et al.
1997
; Mountjoy et al.
2014
,
2018
,
2023
; Ihalainen et al.
2024
).
In the present study, significant decreases in IGF‐1 concentrations from pre to post diet and physical training were observed in all groups. This response to diet and physical training–induced LEA is in line with previous research indicating IGF‐1 as a potential biomarker for identifying at least short‐term LEA (Heikura et al.
2021
). IGF‐1 is a hormone produced primarily by the liver in response to GH stimulation that plays a crucial role in promoting tissue growth, development, and maintenance, whereas the IGF‐1 receptor can be found in all cell types and tissues (Clemmons
2009
). IGF‐1 is involved in muscle protein synthesis, hypertrophy, and inhibition of muscle protein breakdown (Svensson et al.
2003
) making it potentially an important hormone for physique and other athletes. Indeed, in this cohort, IGF‐1 was associated with changes in FFM (Isola et al.
2024
). LEA generally appears to induce GH resistance, which impairs the normal influence of GH on IGF‐1 release from the liver and is reflected by an increase in GH concentrations and a decrease in IGF‐1 concentrations (Fazeli and Klibanski
2014
). This increase in GH and decrease in IGF‐1 appears to require at least short‐term EA at a cutoff of < 30 kcal/kg FFM/d (Loucks et al.
1998
; Loucks and Thuma
2003
; Loucks
2006
; Papageorgiou, Dolan, et al.
2018
; Papageorgiou, Martin, et al.
2018
), a cutoff that was, on average, met by the present groups at post. IGF‐1 was significantly higher in progestin‐only users and naturally menstruating females than combined HC users at recovery. Significant differences in concentrations of IGF‐1 between naturally menstruating females and combined HC users have been reported in the literature (Massa et al.
1993
; Riechman and Lee
2022
; Özcan et al.
2024
), but these observations are not consistent (Massa et al.
1993
). Furthermore, as previously mentioned, combined HC users increased their EA at recovery to a greater degree than progestin‐only HC users and naturally menstruating females, which corresponded with a significant increase in IGF‐1 concentrations. Nevertheless, IGF‐1 concentrations in combined HC users did not increase to the level of progestin‐only HC users and naturally menstruating females at recovery.
Cortisol concentrations did not change significantly over time in any of the groups; however, concentrations of cortisol in combined HC users were generally higher than those of progestin‐only HC users and naturally menstruating females in line with previous research (Hertel et al.
2017
; Masama et al.
2022
), although this finding is not consistent (Kirschbaum et al.
1996
; Larsen et al.
2020
). Our lack of significant change in cortisol concentrations may reflect that HPA axis homeostasis was maintained despite diet and physical training–induced LEA. In the context of physique sports, muscle tissue remodeling (and associated improvements in strength) depends on, at least partially, the balance between anabolic and catabolic hormones. As such, the higher concentrations of cortisol in combined HC users could be of interest, although there does not appear to be robust evidence that combined HC use alone markedly affects training adaptations (Thompson et al.
2020
; Elliott‐Sale et al.
2020
). On the other hand, the possible synergistic effects of HC use, LEA, and strength training have not been thoroughly investigated.
Significant decreases in T3 concentrations were observed from pre to post in all groups. A decrease in T3 may already be observed after short‐term (4 days) LEA of between 19.0 and 25.0 kcal · kg LBM
−1
· day
−1
(Loucks and Heath
1994
) whereas amenorrhea and suspected long‐term LEA were associated with lower T3, sex hormones, and bone mineral density in athletes (Heikura et al.
2018
). Interestingly, concentrations of T3 remained significantly lower at recovery than pre in progestin‐only users and naturally menstruating females who continued to have LEA (based on the cutoff of 30 kcal·kg
−1
FFM·day
−1
) at recovery. The lack of differences between groups is in line with research suggesting that the free fraction of T3 appears to be only slightly changed or unchanged by combined HC use (Torre et al.
2020
) and underscores the potential for T3 to respond in a comparable manner to LEA in HC users and nonusers. Maintaining adequate levels of T3 is important for regulating processes such as metabolism, growth, and development, which are important for weight management, muscle function, and recovery in athletes (A. C. Hackney et al.
2016
). Interestingly, free T3 was generally higher in combined HC users than in progestin‐only HC users and naturally menstruating females. Why this finding occurred is unclear, and we recommend future researchers pursue research addressing this issue.
In the present study, significant decreases in leptin concentrations from pre to post were also observed in all groups. Leptin helps regulate the energy balance via suppression of hunger (Al‐Hussaniy et al.
2021
). The satiety hormone leptin is upregulated when fat mass increases or when energy balance is positive and downregulated when EA decreases or energy balance is negative or energy reserves are low. Leptin is known to decrease already in short‐term LEA, that is, LEA appears to be a stronger stimulus for decreasing leptin than exercise stress (Hilton and Loucks
2000
). The decrease in leptin acts through the hypothalamus potentially increasing appetite and decreasing energy expenditure (Ahlma et al.
1996
), while the consequences of this decrease include suppression of the HPO axis (Childs et al.
2021
) and the HPT axis, as observed in the present study, as well as activation of the HPA axis (Ahlma et al.
1996
). Leptin increased significantly in all groups during recovery but remained significantly lower at recovery than pre in naturally menstruating females who continued to have LEA (based on the cutoff of 30 kcal·kg
−1
FFM·day
−1
) at recovery.
Taken together, it appears that combined HC users maintained concentrations of tT and already suppressed endogenous E2, that is, hormones directly associated with the HPO axis while combined HC and progestin‐only HC users experienced comparable endocrine changes to participants with natural cycles in hormones not directly regulated by the HPO axis (IGF‐1, cortisol, triiodothyronine, and leptin). These results suggest that combined HC use during training and diet‐induced LEA might maintain suppressed HPO axis function whereas other hormonal axes might respond to these combined stressors in a comparable manner irrespective of HC use.
While this is among the first, if not the first, study to report the effects of LEA on hormonal concentrations in HC users, several limitations exist. The observations herein have been made in a relatively homogeneous sample of physique athletes; thus, further research in other athlete populations is needed. The data were extracted from two physique athlete studies that were not specifically designed to answer the present research question. As such, for example, menstrual cycle or HC use phases were not controlled for, or recorded, and changes in menstrual status were not monitored or tracked, although basic information about the HC brand/dose/type was provided by participants and reported herein. Importantly, controlling for menstrual cycle or HC phases was not practical in the present study design, as competitions cannot be arranged for individuals based on their cycles. While we acknowledge this limitation, the patterns observed on a group level in terms of E2, tT, IGF‐1, cortisol, T3, and leptin are not entirely menstrual cycle or HC cycle phase‐dependent, suggesting that the reported observations are valid. We also acknowledge that the number of hormones investigated was relatively limited; however, we were able to report the major hormones representing the HPO (estradiol and testosterone), HPA (cortisol), HPT (triiodothyronine), GH‐IGF‐1 (IGF‐1), and adipose tissue (leptin) axes and provide evidence suggesting that further investigations of the endocrine system in the triad and REDs paradigms consider HC use. With regard to HPO axis function, it is worth noting that E2 is already suppressed in combined HC users where a further decrease in this endogenous hormone may be difficult to meaningfully detect. Finally, our results are limited by the sample sizes of the groups examined, but we feel that the novelty of this paper mitigates this limitation.
7
Conclusions
Our results indicate that already suppressed HPO axis function may be maintained in combined HC users in the presence of LEA, as indicated by the observed lack of changes in E2 and tT. However, significant decreases in IGF‐1, T3, and leptin indicate that combined and progestin‐only HCs may experience other potentially negative endocrine consequences of LEA. Our results imply that the synergistic effects of combined HC use and diet and physical training–induced LEA require attention in both applied and clinical research. While our results need to be confirmed, they do indicate that the endocrine consequences of LEA in HC users should be considered in future well‐controlled research regarding LEA, REDs, and the female athlete triad. While the differences observed in hormone concentrations between groups might suggest the possibility for cutoff values for hormonal indicators of LEA/REDs based on the HC type, this may not be practical due to the variety of HCs available; thus, such cutoffs require additional research. Finally, both HC users and physicians prescribing HCs should be made aware that diet and physical training–induced LEA can negatively affect endocrine health also in HC users.
