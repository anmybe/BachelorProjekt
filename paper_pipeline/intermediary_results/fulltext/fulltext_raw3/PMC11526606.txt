Lung injury is closely associated with the heterogeneity, severity, mortality, and prognosis of various respiratory diseases. Effective monitoring of lung injury is crucial for the optimal management and improved outcomes of patients with lung diseases. This review describes acute and chronic respiratory diseases characterized by significant lung injury and current clinical tools for assessing lung health. Furthermore, we summarized the mechanisms of lung cell death observed in these diseases and highlighted recently identified biomarkers in the plasma indicative of injury to specific cell types and scaffold structure in the lung. Last, we propose an artificial intelligence-driven lung injury monitoring model to assess disease severity, and predict mortality and prognosis, aiming to achieve precision and personalized medicine.

Introduction
Acute and chronic lung diseases pose significant health challenges and economic burdens, impacting both severity and prognosis primarily through lung injury. Despite clinical focus on overall lung alterations, diagnosis relies heavily on symptomatic, morphological, and functional assessments. These include imaging for indirect lung morphology observation, lung function tests, arterial blood gas analysis, and non-invasive oxygen saturation measurements to assess gas exchange [
1
,
2
]. Pathological examinations aid in disease elucidation but often lack specificity regarding injury location, affected cell types, and injury extent. This limitation complicates the development of precise, personalized treatment strategies.
Lung injury encompasses damage to epithelial and endothelial cells, along with the extracellular scaffold supporting these cells. This damage triggers the release of cellular proteins, activating resident inflammatory cells, increasing vascular permeability, and promoting inflammatory cell infiltration into the lungs. While essential for immune defense, inflammatory cells can exacerbate cellular damage. Circulating proteins from damaged cells offer a means to precisely monitor lung cellular and structural damage by analyzing cell-specific protein expression levels in serum. Concurrent detection of oxidative irritants or cytokines contributing to lung cell damage further enhances diagnostic capabilities. Recent advancements in omics technologies have significantly improved the identification of lung injury biomarkers [
3
]. This review will summarize lung injury characteristics across common lung diseases, elucidate molecular mechanisms of injury, and catalog specific serum biomarkers indicative of inflammatory, oxidative, epithelial, endothelial, and extracellular scaffold damage in the lung. Pathogenesis and biomarkers of lung remodeling and fibrosis are not the focus of this review, as these topics have been comprehensively covered in recent literature [
3
–
7
]. Additionally, it will propose an integrated lung injury evaluation platform, aiming to amalgamate these biomarkers with clinical indicators and demographic data, utilizing artificial intelligence to accurately assess disease severity, predict patient outcomes, and determine long-term prognosis.
Respiratory diseases with lung injury
In acute respiratory infections, various viral and bacterial pathogens such as SARS-CoV-2, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), influenza,
Streptococcus pneumoniae
, and
Mycobacterium tuberculosis
lead to lung injury by targeting specific cell types within the lung (Fig.
1
). Respiratory viruses invade epithelial cells and endothelial cells, triggering cell death mechanisms like apoptosis, pyroptosis, or necrosis. This process releases pro-inflammatory cytokines, exacerbating inflammation and compromising the airway and air-blood barrier integrity. Inflammatory responses upregulate leukocyte adhesion molecules, promoting immune cell accumulation and worsening lung function. Bacterial infections cause pneumonia characterized by pulmonary edema, inflammatory exudates, and recruitment of immune cells, which further disrupts the air-blood barrier and leads to systemic infection in severe cases [
8
]. Chronic infections may lead to bronchiectasis and irreversible airway dilation. Neutrophils, macrophages, and eosinophils predominate in lung inflammation, generating ROS, cytokines, and NETs that contribute to ongoing lung scaffold damage and mucus hypersecretion [
9
]. Apoptosis of inflammatory cells and epithelial cells like ciliated and club cells worsens airway integrity, perpetuating the disease cycle.
In chronic lung disease such as asthma, allergen or irritant-induced inflammation causes airway epithelial shedding, mucin production, and remodeling. The epithelial layer is compromised, with increased apoptosis of ciliated cells and impaired repair processes, leading to airway hyperresponsiveness (Fig.
1
). Eosinophil-mediated injury of club cells contributes to disease severity of asthma [
10
,
11
]. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) encompasses bronchitis, fibrosis and emphysema attributing to three variant lung epithelial progenitor respectively, is also found with apoptosis of club cells, ciliated cells, AT1 cells, AT2 cells, and endothelial cells [
12
]. Inflammatory cells release neutrophil elastase and matrix metalloproteinase (MMPs), disrupting the lung's proteinase/anti-proteinase balance and causing the degradation of lung elastin and connective tissue [
13
]. ECM remodeling and altered collagen deposition further contribute to lung function decline. Interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and connective tissue disease-associated ILD (CTD-ILD) involve fibroblast proliferation, ECM deposition, and myofibroblast differentiation. TGF-β signaling upregulation drives fibrogenesis, leading to irreversible lung fibrosis, AT1 and AT2 cell loss, and alveolar epithelial stem cell dysfunction [
14
]. These changes exacerbate lung injury, compromising respiratory function and prognosis in affected individuals.
Clinical markers indicative of lung injury
Symptoms such as cough with phlegm suggest upper airway involvement, common in infections, bronchitis, and pneumonia, while chest tightness, pain, and shortness of breath indicate alveolar issues like fluid accumulation or pulmonary embolism [
15
]. Abnormal breath sounds like wheezes indicate airway obstruction (e.g., asthma), while crackles signify fluid or secretion in airways (e.g., bronchitis, pneumonia). Velcro crackles denote diffuse interstitial pulmonary fibrosis [
16
]. Lung percussion reveals resonant sounds normally, hyperresonance in emphysema, and dullness in conditions like pleural effusion [
17
]. Imaging techniques like X-rays and CT scans show low attenuation in emphysema and high attenuation in inflammatory states, consolidation, or general interstitial lung abnormalities [
18
,
19
]. Lung function tests detect obstructive patterns (e.g., reduced FEV1, FEV1/FVC ratio) and restrictive patterns (e.g., reduced lung capacities), while blood gas analysis assesses gas exchange impairments like low PaO
2
indicating ventilation issues and elevated PaCO
2
indicating gas exchange problems [
20
]. OI values < 300 mmHg suggest acute lung injury, < 100 mmHg may indicate ARDS. These assessments provide insights into lung conditions but do not fully capture cellular injury or predict disease outcomes, highlighting the need for comprehensive monitoring strategies.
Molecular mechanisms of lung injury
Viral infection-induced cell death
Viruses kill cells, resulting in the release of intracellular proteins. Influenza A virus (IAV) infects airway and alveolar epithelial cells by binding to sialic acid residues, inducing viral fusion with cell membranes and subsequent replication, causing cell injury (Fig.
2
) [
21
]. IAV triggers necroptosis through the ZBP1/RIPK3/MLKL pathway and apoptosis via caspase-8 activation and inflammatory cytokines, leading to epithelial cell death [
22
]. SARS-CoV-2 utilizes ACE2 to enter AT2 and lung endothelial cells, activating multiple cell death pathways. Its spike protein induces pyroptosis via NLRP3, NLRP1, and NLRC4 inflammasomes, caspase-1/GSDME pathway [
23
], and apoptosis via caspase-8 activation by membrane and nucleocapsid proteins [
24
]. Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) enters ciliated and endothelial cells via CX3CR1, causing apoptosis due to endoplasmic reticulum stress. Adenovirus infects alveolar epithelial cells through coxsackievirus-adenovirus receptors, inducing apoptosis via caspase activation. The SARS-CoV-2, RSV and adenovirus also triggers necroptosis through the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL pathway [
25
]. Herpes simplex virus targets alveolar epithelial cells through glycoprotein receptors, inducing necroptosis via RIPK1-RIPK3 pathway, apoptosis via death ligands like TNF-α and TRAIL interacting with TNFR, and caspase activation [
26
,
27
].
Bacterial infection-induced cell death
Bacteria do not kill cells directly.
Mycobacterium tuberculosis
infection initiates tuberculosis by forming granulomas, where the pathogen interacts with host receptors like TLR, facilitating dissemination and inducing cell death mechanisms in infected cells (Fig.
2
) [
28
]. In alveoli,
Mycobacterium tuberculosis
evades immune responses by triggering apoptosis in AT2 cells and macrophages.
Pseudomonas aeruginosa
infection induces apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells via CD18 integrin-mediated entry, activating caspase-3 and caspase-8, leading to lung injury, ARDS, and compromised air-blood barrier integrity [
29
]. Lung endothelial damage from
Pseudomonas aeruginosa
triggers inflammation and cell death through caspase-1 activation [
30
].
Streptococcus pneumoniae
and
Staphylococcus aureus
induce necroptosis in lung epithelial cells and macrophages via the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL pathway [
31
].
Streptococcus pneumoniae
enters lung epithelial cells through surface phosphorylcholine and choline-binding protein A, producing hydrogen peroxide that disrupts cellular function, leading to cytoskeletal and mitochondrial dysfunction and subsequent cell death [
32
].
Oxidative stress-induced cell death
ROS damage essential cellular components like proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, ultimately leading to cell death. In the lungs, ROS activate the extrinsic apoptosis pathway by engaging death receptors such as Fas, TRAIL-R1/2, and TNFR, resulting in caspase-8 activation and subsequent apoptosis (Fig.
2
) [
33
]. Mitochondrial dysfunction is involved in ROS-induced apoptosis. ROS accumulation triggers NLRP3 inflammasomes, initiating pyroptosis in airway and alveolar epithelial cells, as well as endothelial cells in lung vasculature [
34
,
35
]. ROS accumulation also triggers ferroptosis in lung epithelial cells, an iron-dependent cell death mechanism initiated by lipid peroxidation of membrane phospholipid’s polyunsaturated fatty acid tails [
36
]. Various enzymes catalyze oxidative reactions causing lipid peroxidation, with electron leakage generating hydrogen peroxide that oxidizes ferrous iron to hydroxyl radicals, promoting lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. Furthermore, ROS accumulation induces necroptosis in airway epithelial (ciliated and club cells) and alveolar epithelial cells (AT1 and AT2 cells) [
37
,
38
]. ROS regulate necroptosis through phosphorylation of RIPK1, a key pro-necroptotic molecule. ROS-induced RIPK1 phosphorylation activates RIPK1 and RIPK3, which interact to promote necroptotic cell death [
39
].
Cytokine-induced cell death
Cytokines play a critical role in cell death processes, contributing to inflammatory cascades that exacerbate lung tissue damage and compromise the air-blood barrier, often leading to cytokine storms (Fig.
2
) [
40
]. TNF-α and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) are central in these processes: TNF-α and Fas ligand induce apoptosis via Complex I and cytoplasmic activation of caspases, resulting in cell death characterized by chromatin fragmentation and apoptotic body formation [
41
]. TNF-α upregulation is observed in severe lung conditions, while IFN-γ, crucial for viral defense, promotes necroptosis of alveolar and airway epithelial cells by activating MLKL [
42
]. IL-6 plays a dual role in lung injury and repair. It supports lung stem cell repair but also drives a pro-inflammatory response, stimulating tissue factor production that triggers the coagulation cascade, potentially leading to microthrombus formation and disseminated intravascular coagulation, impairing lung function [
43
]. IL-8 recruits neutrophils, promoting NET formation and immune thrombosis, which can cause lung cell death and extensive neutrophil infiltration [
44
]. IL-8 also affects neutrophil apoptosis and interacts with pulmonary endothelial cells, contributing to lung injury. Inflammasome-induced IL-1β and IL-18 during pyroptosis of lung epithelial and endothelial cells further disrupt endothelial barrier integrity, increasing pulmonary permeability [
45
,
46
]. These complex interactions highlight the pivotal role of cytokines in lung pathology, influencing both inflammatory responses and tissue repair mechanisms.
Biomarkers of lung injury
Epithelial injury biomarkers
Club cells
Club cell secretory protein (CCSP), or CC16 or CC10, is specifically expressed by club cells and immuno-protective in airways. With its anti-inflammatory properties, CCSP contributes to host defense against various external stimuli such as environmental factors, infections, oxidative stress, and inflammation (Fig.
3
) [
47
]. Excess CCSP is released when club cells are injured. In ARDS, increased permeability of the pulmonary capillary-alveolar membrane leads to elevated plasma levels of CCSP, which can predict mortality rates in patients with ARDS [
48
]. Elevated CCSP concentrations were observed in patients with COVID-19, as well as in those with sarcoidosis and pulmonary fibrosis, due to club cell injury and increased permeability and leakage of the air-blood barrier [
49
,
50
].
AT1 cells
The receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) is predominantly expressed on AT1 cells. MMP-9 cleaves RAGE, releasing soluble RAGE (sRAGE) into the capillary-alveolar membrane interstitium. sRAGE also includes endogenously secreted RAGE from selective splicing. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) binding to RAGE triggers pro-inflammatory responses, causing alveolar epithelial cell death, a key mechanism in ARDS. Elevated plasma sRAGE is evident in pediatric and adult ARDS due to AT1 cell injury and air-blood barrier integrity loss [
51
]. Increased sRAGE correlates with enhanced pulmonary MMP-9 activity during ARDS. LPS exposure upregulates RAGE and HMGB1 in mouse models. Lower sRAGE levels in ILD and IPF suggest AT1 cell loss in these diseases, emphasizing sRAGE as a disease-specific marker for AT1 cell injury [
52
,
53
].
AT2 cells
Pulmonary surfactant protein-D (SP-D), synthesized primarily by AT2 cells, is released into the alveolar space and circulation upon AT2 cell injury. During acute lung injury, neutrophils degrade SP-D, increasing fragment levels in the alveolar space. Elevated plasma SP-D levels are noted in COVID-19 patients, correlating with mortality risk in A/H1N1 infections and severity of pediatric ARDS [
54
]. Plasma SP-D levels also rise in IPF and COPD [
55
,
56
]. Chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 1 (CRTAC-1), identified as another AT2 cell marker via single-cell transcriptomics, shows reduced levels in BALF and plasma during AT2 cell injury. Studies indicate CRTAC-1 downregulation in severe COVID-19, possibly due to disrupted AT2 cell positioning on the basement membrane, affecting CRTAC-1 secretion into plasma [
57
,
58
].
Pan-epithelial cells
Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6) is a transmembrane protein found on AT2 and bronchial epithelial cells. Elevated plasma KL-6 levels indicate AT2 cell damage and are higher in IPF and COVID-19 patients [
59
]. A meta-analysis ranks lung epithelial biomarkers, highlighting KL-6 with the largest effect size in identifying high-risk patients [
48
]. Cytokeratin 18 (CK18) released from necrotic epithelial cells indicates apoptosis; ELISA detects its M30 fragment and intact CK18-M65 protein, indicating epithelial cell death [
60
]. CYFRA 21-1, released after proteolytic cleavage during lung epithelial cell apoptosis or necrosis, serves as a marker in circulation and airways [
61
]. Elevated CK-18 levels in BALF correlate with ARDS severity, and plasma CYFRA21-1 levels are higher in severe ARDS and COVID-19 patients [
62
]. Serum CYFRA21-1 associates with systemic sclerosis-ILD severity, highlighting CK18, CK19, and their fragments as general lung epithelial cell markers [
63
].
Endothelial injury biomarkers
Circulating endothelial cells (CECs) and endothelial microparticles (EMPs) are critical markers of endothelial injury in a spectrum of lung diseases (Fig.
3
). CECs, detached from damaged vessels during inflammatory, infectious, or ischemic conditions, exhibit elevated counts in diseases such as COVID-19, ARDS, and COPD, reflecting severe endothelial damage and vascular dysfunction [
64
]. EMPs, originating from activated or apoptotic endothelial cells under the influence of cytokines, endotoxins, and thrombin, significantly increase during pathological states including septic ARDS, asthma exacerbations, and exacerbating COPD, correlating closely with disease severity and endothelial injury [
65
,
66
].
Angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2), a marker of vascular permeability, is markedly elevated in COVID-19, acute exacerbations of COPD, and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), indicating disrupted endothelial barrier function and contributing to disease progression [
67
]. The endothelial glycocalyx, crucial for maintaining vascular integrity and function, undergoes degradation during acute lung injury (ALI) induced by inflammatory mediators like TNF-α and LPS. This results in the shedding of glycocalyx components such as syndecan-1, hyaluronic acid, and heparan sulfate into plasma, with elevated levels predicting severe ARDS, COPD exacerbations, and IPF or connective tissue disease-associated interstitial lung disease (CTD-ILD) [
68
–
70
].
Endothelin-1 (ET-1), essential for vascular tone regulation, increases in response to inflammatory cytokines in COVID-19 and correlates with impaired lung function in patients with IPF, rheumatoid arthritis-associated interstitial lung disease (RA-ILD), and COPD [
71
,
72
]. Von Willebrand Factor (VWF), released from endothelial cells upon injury, is upregulated in COVID-19 and COPD, indicative of vascular damage and immune cell extravasation into lung tissues [
73
,
74
].
Selectins (E-selectin, P-selectin) and cell adhesion molecules (ICAM-1, VCAM-1), pivotal in leukocyte recruitment during inflammation, are elevated on endothelial surfaces in conditions such as COVID-19, RA-ILD, and IPF. These molecules are cleaved from endothelial cells by MMPs and released into circulation, contributing to the inflammatory milieu and disease progression [
75
,
76
]. Soluble thrombomodulin (sTM), known for its anti-inflammatory and anticoagulant properties, increases in plasma during COVID-19 and ARDS, correlating with higher mortality rates and adverse outcomes in IPF [
77
,
78
]. Plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), a key regulator of fibrinolysis, rises in response to endothelial injury in COVID-19 and IPF, promoting thrombus formation and contributing to disease severity in COPD [
79
].
Collectively, these endothelial biomarkers serve as vital indicators of endothelial dysfunction and injury severity across diverse lung diseases. They provide valuable insights into disease mechanisms and may guide the development of targeted therapies aimed at preserving endothelial function and improving patient outcomes.
Extracellular scaffold damage biomarkers
Laminin, a vital extracellular scaffold glycoprotein secreted by alveolar epithelial cells, forms a heterotrimeric matrix protein critical for cell adhesion, growth, and repair (Fig.
3
). In COVID-19, elevated plasma laminin persists up to 6 months post-discharge, indicating sustained epithelial and scaffold injury [
80
]. Moreover, laminin γ2 chain fragments (G2F) are significantly elevated ALI but decrease in survivors, underscoring its prognostic relevance. Similarly, elevated serum laminin levels associate with ILD and IPF [
70
], suggesting impaired epithelial repair mechanisms in these conditions.
Endostatin, a collagen-derived peptide, functions as a potent endogenous inhibitor of angiogenesis. Elevated plasma endostatin levels correlate with mortality rates in severe COVID-19 cases and are also significantly increased in COPD, indicating its role in endothelial damage and disease severity across various lung disorders [
81
,
82
]. The release of endostatin during ARDS reflects basement membrane fragmentation due to vascular injury and subsequent inflammatory responses.
MMPs are pivotal enzymes involved in ECM remodeling and degradation. MMP-1, MMP-3, MMP-7, MMP-9, and MMP-12 degrade various ECM components, contributing to tissue damage and inflammation. Elevated plasma MMPs levels are observed in COPD, COVID-19, IPF, and ILD, underscoring their role in disease pathogenesis and progression [
83
,
84
]. Tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) regulate MMP activity, maintaining ECM homeostasis. Fibroblasts, macrophages, and epithelial cells in the lungs primarily express TIMPs. Like MMPs, TIMPs levels in plasma are elevated in ARDS, COVID-19, COPD, and IPF [
85
,
86
]. Inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and TGF-β promote TIMP-1 expression. Elevated TIMP-1 levels are linked to fibrosis, whereas lower levels are associated with proteolysis and fluid leakage [
87
]. Osteopontin (OPN), expressed primarily by alveolar macrophages and fibroblasts, is a multifunctional glycoprotein involved in ECM remodeling and inflammation. OPN levels are elevated in COVID-19 and COPD, correlating with disease severity and outcomes [
88
]. Its upregulation reflects its role in promoting inflammation, tissue injury, and fibrosis within the lung microenvironment.
These biomarkers collectively reflect the intricate interplay between ECM dynamics, immune responses, and disease progression in various lung disease. Understanding their roles provides insights into potential therapeutic targets aimed at preserving lung structure and mitigating lung injury outcomes.
Inflammatory biomarkers
TNF-α acts as a chemoattractant for neutrophils and eosinophils, boosting the production of chemokines like CCL2 by macrophages, thereby facilitating inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig.
3
) [
89
]. Furthermore, TNF-α promotes vasodilation by releasing arachidonic acid and platelet-activating factors. The binding of TNF-α to its receptor induces apoptosis of epithelial and endothelial cells in alveoli. Plasma TNF-α levels in intensive care (ICU) patients with COVID-19 are higher than non-ICU patients [
90
]. Similarly, elevated TNF-α levels have been observed in patients with ARDS, ILD, and COPD [
91
,
92
]. Despite extensive research on its inflammatory roles, less attention has been devoted to TNF-α’s impact on lung repair.
Fas/FasL signaling induces apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells, resulting in proteinaceous edema within the alveolar interstitium. Fas/FasL also triggers the activation of neutrophils to mediate dysregulated immune responses and lung injury [
93
]. Soluble FasL is generated when membrane-anchored FasL is cleaved by zinc-regulated MMPs. Mild COVID-19 cases exhibit increased sFas levels. In contrast, severe cases show lower levels relative to both control and mild COVID-19, potentially due to their consumption during neutrophil activity and recruitment in the lungs during the inflammatory phase [
94
]. Additionally, plasma levels of Fas/FasL are elevated in COPD [
95
].
Elevated plasma IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-8 are characteristic of COPD and early IPF and correlates with the severity of COVID-19 and IPF [
96
,
97
]. IL-1β enhances lung inflammation and fibrosis by upregulating chemokines and adhesion molecules in endothelial and stromal cells, promoting immune cell infiltration and neutrophilia. Activation of NLRP3 inflammasome by SARS-CoV and LPS increases IL-1β production [
98
]. IL-6 mediates pro-inflammatory functions and increases vascular permeability enhancing systemic circulation entry. Despite controversies, IL-6 balances inflammation and lung repair, showing potential in reducing influenza-induced injury [
99
]. IL-8 recruits neutrophils to lungs via CXCR1/2 interaction. IL-18 increases vascular permeability, leading to tissue damage and microthrombus formation, evident in ARDS, COVID-19, and COPD [
100
,
101
].
As calcium-binding proteins, S100A8/S100A9 are major DAMPs released during lung injury. Comprehensive proteomic analyses of lung and plasma samples from LPS-induced ARDS mice and patients with COVID-19-related ARDS revealed a significant increase in S100A9 levels [
102
], with plasma S100A9/S100A8 levels correlating with disease severity of COVID-19 [
103
]. Elevated serum S100A8/S100A9 levels are also seen in patients with COPD and IPF, with a negative correlation between patients with COPD lung function and S100A8/S100A9 levels [
104
]. Inflamed lung tissues release IL-17, which provokes neutrophils to produce S100A8/A9, inducing apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells.
Oxidative injury biomarkers
Inflammatory lung diseases exhibit ROS accumulation due to an imbalance between oxidative and antioxidative systems. This imbalance may result in neutrophil aggregation, enhanced secretion of proteases, and the formation of oxidative intermediates, contributing to cell apoptosis and lung tissue damage (Fig.
3
). Endogenous sources of ROS generation include mitochondria, peroxisomes, various types of oxidases, ascorbate oxidase, cytochromes, ultraviolet/ionizing radiation, chemotherapeutic drugs, stress, and environmental toxins. Plasma ROS levels are increased in COVID-19. Serum ROS levels are also elevated in COPD and negatively correlated with lung function parameters [
105
]. While low levels of ROS typically act as signaling molecules, high levels of ROS impair lung repair and induce cell apoptosis. Several biomarkers can effectively monitor oxidative stress. These biomarkers include antioxidants (e.g., vitamins A, C, D, and E), enzyme antioxidants (e.g., endogenous glutathione, glutathione peroxidase, superoxide dismutase), and lipid oxidation end-product malondialdehyde. The ratio of oxidant to antioxidant determines the extent of oxidative damage.
Other emerging lung injury biomarkers
Circulating molecules such as nucleic acids, extracellular vesicles (EVs), and blood metabolites are increasingly recognized as biomarkers of lung injury. Key nucleic acid biomarkers include circulating free DNA (cfDNA), mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), circulating microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs). cfDNA, composed of short DNA fragments primarily derived from mononucleosomes, is released during cellular injury, death, or renewal, while mtDNA is released from damaged mitochondria. lncRNAs are transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides that lack protein-coding capacity. In ARDS patients, plasma mtDNA levels are significantly elevated [
106
,
107
]. miR-29a-3p, miR-150, and miR-1 are notably downregulated, while miR-122 is associated with disease severity [
108
,
109
]. Elevated expression of lncRNAs such as AC000120.7 and SENP3-EIF4A1, and circRNA circ_2979 has also been reported in ARDS plasma [
110
,
111
]. EVs, lipid bilayer-enclosed extracellular particles released by various cell types, mediate intercellular communication. Increased serum EV levels, particularly those derived from platelets, have been observed in ARDS [
112
]. Metabolomic analysis has revealed that reduced sphingosine-1-phosphate correlates with ARDS prognosis [
113
], highlighting the potential role of amino acids, lipids, and energy production pathways in disease severity and clinical outcomes.
Importance of artificial intelligence-directed lung injury evaluation
In a meta-analysis involving 2654 ARDS patients, the predictive performance of CCSP, SP-D, and KL-6 for ARDS mortality was found to be suboptimal, with SP-D demonstrating the lowest predictive ability [
114
]. In contrast, the combination of ferritin and KL-6 as biomarkers showed significantly greater predictive efficacy for the progression to severe ARDS compared to any individual biomarker [
115
]. This underscores that a single biomarker is inadequate to capture the complexity of the disease, which is influenced not only by comorbidities but also by the intricate regulation of biomarker expression.
The integrated analysis of laboratory tests, digital radiography imaging, and pathological results has always been fundamental in clinical practice. However, the varying impact of each test on disease diagnosis and subsequent treatment can lead to heterogeneity in disease understanding and poor patient outcomes. Artificial intelligence (AI) excels in data analysis and intelligent decision-making from large datasets and has demonstrated powerful capabilities in many medical fields. In respiratory diseases, AI applications range from automated image analysis in radiology to predictive modeling for disease progression and treatment responses. AI can improve diagnostic accuracy, personalize treatment plans, and streamline workflows, improving patient management and outcomes. For example, AI algorithms can analyze chest CT scans to detect early signs of lung cancer or predict exacerbations in patients with COPD [
116
,
117
]. A machine learning classifier, trained on 13 blood biomarkers from 455 patients with IPF, identified three distinct clusters [
118
]. Notably, the epithelial injury cluster was associated with the highest risk of mortality and the most substantial decline in lung function. For the first time, biologically informed neural networks were developed to integrate biological pathway analysis with biomarker identification, enabling differentiation of ARDS from various etiologies [
119
]. The use of AI in respiratory disease management represents a significant advancement towards precision medicine.
The heterogeneity of respiratory diseases is related to the location and extent of lung injury. The discovery of plasma biomarkers enhances existing clinical methods for assessing lung injury, promising more accurate localization and evaluating its impact on disease progression and prognosis. However, traditional methods of integrating these biomarkers with clinical examinations based on personal experience have not achieved breakthroughs in lung injury monitoring. Consequently, these biomarkers have yet to become a staple in the decision-making process of pulmonologists. To translate these scientific discoveries into practical medicine, neural networks and other machine-learning techniques are essential. Machine learning can integrate vast amounts of patient medical information, including demographics, clinical examinations, and plasma biomarkers, to establish an intelligent model for lung injury monitoring (Fig.
4
). This model must correlate with drug prescription, disease progression, outcomes, and long-term sequelae, thereby enabling accurate assessment of disease severity and prognosis and facilitating personalized and precise respiratory care.
Analyzing proteins, metabolites, and microorganisms in BALF can further refine this lung injury assessment model. However, obtaining BALF samples can be invasive, costly, and sometimes impractical. Therefore, peripheral blood-based laboratory tests and biomarker identification maximize patient benefits while reducing the burden on physicians. AI-directed lung injury monitoring model may predict the incidence of severity and mortality risk and warn medical staff to implement timely interventions to avoid severe progression and reduce mortality, especially in patients with ARDS or advanced chronic respiratory diseases. This model allows physicians to develop personalized treatment plans by selecting the most suitable drugs and treatment methods and adjusting treatment doses and frequencies. In addition to identifying hidden respiratory diseases during physical examinations, this model may also include follow-up data to develop personalized follow-up and treatment plans. Ultimately, this approach streamlines workflow and ensures patients receive optimized care based on the most advanced, data-driven insights.
Concluding remarks and future perspectives
Lung injury manifests with varying degrees of damage to the epithelium, endothelium, and extracellular scaffold, significantly impacting the severity, mortality, and prognosis of acute and chronic respiratory diseases. Clinical practice increasingly integrates clinical examinations with emerging biomarkers using AI to establish precise lung injury monitoring models. These models provide real-time insights into lung pathological status and disease progression. While ciliated cell markers remain elusive in lung diseases, emerging soluble factors involved in lung epithelial and endothelial cell repair and regeneration show promise for inclusion in injury assessment. Future directions should incorporate multi-omics metabolic profiling to advance biomarker discovery comprehensively. This approach spans epigenomic insights for gene expression prediction, transcriptomics for gene variant expression levels, proteomics for mRNA translation assessment, and metabolomics for protein functionality evaluation. Integrating these insights with the latest clinical and experimental data will refine and validate lung injury biomarkers continually. This iterative process enhances real-time monitoring capabilities and optimizes AI-driven models for lung injury assessment. Deep learning-based models derived from comprehensive data analyses significantly enhance diagnostic accuracy, refine treatment strategies, predict and mitigate severe cases, and improve patient survival and quality of life. By facilitating precise early identification of severe respiratory cases, timely intervention, and accurate prognosis assessment, healthcare providers can effectively manage patients with respiratory diseases.
