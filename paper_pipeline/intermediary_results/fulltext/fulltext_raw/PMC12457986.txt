Abstract
INTRODUCTION
Cognitive decline in asymptomatic preclinical Alzheimer's disease (AD) is slow and variable, limiting detection of treatment effects. This study developed models to forecast trajectories and improve trial efficiency.
METHODS
Models were trained on longitudinal Preclinical Alzheimer's Cognitive Composite (PACC) data up to 240 weeks from the Phase III A4 study of solanezumab. Baseline inputs included demographics, apolipoprotein E (
APOE
) ε4, clinical scores, amyloid positron emission tomography (PET), plasma pTau217, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and tau PET (sub‐study). Stochastic gradient boosting was used, with evaluation via cross‐validation and trial simulations.
RESULTS
The best model without tau PET used pTau217, clinical, and MRI data (
R
2
= 0.32; area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) for classifying a 0.5‐point PACC decline = 78.6%). Replacing MRI with tau PET improved performance (
R
2
= 0.42; AUROC = 83.1%). Predicted trajectories as a prognostic covariate reduced sample sizes by 35% and increased power from 80% to 94.7%.
DISCUSSION
Prognostic models can predict decline in preclinical AD and improve trial efficiency.

INTRODUCTION
Alzheimer's disease (AD) is defined by a prolonged preclinical phase during which individuals exhibit pathological changes, specifically, amyloid‐β accumulation, well before the onset of clinical symptoms.
1
,
2
This preclinical stage provides a crucial opportunity for therapeutic intervention aimed at delaying or preventing symptomatic decline. However, substantial delay and heterogeneity exist in the progression of individuals with biomarker‐confirmed preclinical AD, even among those classified as amyloid‐positive.
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
This variability, along with the characteristically slow rate of cognitive decline in early stages, creates challenges for clinical trial design by increasing uncertainty in treatment effect estimation. As a result, trials require longer durations, large sample sizes, and may suffer from reduced statistical power.
8
,
9
Recent advances in fluid and imaging biomarkers have improved the identification and staging of biological AD.
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
These markers can be leveraged to forecast future clinical trajectories at the individual level. A growing body of evidence suggests that baseline multimodal data, including plasma phosphorylated tau‐217 (pTau217), amyloid, and tau positron emission tomography (PET), and structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), contain prognostic information about future cognitive decline.
3

Recent studies have successfully developed prognostic models from research cohorts and historical controls, capable of predicting longitudinal clinical progression trajectories in early AD.
27
,
28
,
29
Incorporating these models into trial designs and analysis may enhance efficiency and improve statistical power without narrowing inclusion criteria or representativeness of the clinical trial population.
30
,
31
,
32
A promising approach to improving trial efficiency involves using model‐based predictions of expected untreated clinical progression from baseline data as an AD prognostic covariate (APC) in the regression model for estimating treatment effects.
33
The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidance supports the use of prognostic covariates associated with clinical outcomes, emphasizing the value of historical data in deriving such covariates.
34
In the Phase II and Phase III trials of lecanemab for early AD, the APC was generated for each participant using a prediction model trained on historical controls with baseline clinical and structural MRI measures.
29
Incorporating the APC into mixed‐effects models for repeated measurements (MMRM) improved precision in estimating treatment effects, reduced bias, and increased statistical power by adjusting for inter‐individual heterogeneity in clinical progression. Simulation studies further revealed that including the APC in MMRM analyses improves statistical power and reduces sample size requirements in early AD trials.
Despite their potential, APCs have not yet been evaluated in preclinical AD populations. This gap is particularly significant because cognitive changes in preclinical AD are typically slower and more variable than in symptomatic stages.
4
,
6
,
35
Data from the Anti‐Amyloid Treatment in Asymptomatic Alzheimer's (A4) Study,
36
which enrolled more than 1100 amyloid‐positive, cognitively unimpaired older adults, provides a unique opportunity to develop models using multimodal baseline data to predict clinical progression trajectories and evaluate their utility as APCs for improving trial efficiency.
RESEARCH IN CONTEXT
Systematic review
: We reviewed prior literature using PubMed and meeting abstracts. Several studies have identified baseline predictors of cognitive decline in preclinical Alzheimer's disease (AD), including plasma pTau217, regional tau positron emission tomography (PET), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measures. However, few have developed models to forecast individual cognitive trajectories or assessed their utility for optimizing clinical trial design in preclinical AD.
Interpretation
: Using longitudinal data from the A4 Study, we developed machine learning models to predict 4.5‐year Preclinical Alzheimer's Cognitive Composite (PACC) trajectories in amyloid‐positive, cognitively unimpaired individuals. When applied as prognostic covariates, model‐derived predictions based on baseline features, such as plasma pTau217 and regional tau PET, substantially increased statistical power and reduced sample size requirements in simulated preclinical AD trials.
Future directions
: Validation in additional cohorts is needed to strengthen generalizability. Future work should assess emerging biomarkers (e.g., plasma eMTBR‐tau243) and quantify the impact of applying model‐derived prognostic covariates in prospective trials targeting preclinical AD.
In this study, we developed machine learning models to predict 4.5‐year trajectories of cognitive decline, as measured by the Preclinical Alzheimer's Cognitive Composite (PACC),
37
using baseline clinical assessments, plasma pTau217, structural MRI, and regional tau PET standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs). These predictions were used to derive APCs. We then simulated their application within an MMRM framework to estimate treatment effects and quantify their impact on statistical power and sample size in hypothetical preclinical AD trials. Building on our prior use of APCs in early AD, this work extends their utility to the earliest clinically identifiable stage of the AD continuum.

METHODS
2.1
Database
Data were obtained from the A4 Study (NCT02008357), a multicenter, placebo‐controlled Phase III clinical trial to evaluate the efficacy of solanezumab in preventing cognitive decline among cognitively normal, amyloid‐positive older adults. The trial, which enrolled 1169 individuals aged 65–86 years with elevated brain amyloid levels, did not demonstrate a statistically significant treatment benefit.
36
For the present analysis, progression models were trained using data from both the placebo (
n
= 519) and treatment arms (
n
= 504), after excluding individuals with missing baseline variables. This resulted in a final sample of 1023 participants for models that did not include tau PET data. For models incorporating tau PET, a subset of 339 participants (placebo:
n
= 170; treatment:
n
= 169) with complete tau imaging data was used. Model training was conducted on the full dataset, with treatment arm assignment included as a predictor.
The A4 Study was approved by the Institutional Review Board or independent ethics committees at each site. All participants and their study partners provided written informed consent before data collection, which included consent for data sharing. A summary of key demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population is provided in Table
1
.
2.2
Clinical assessments, imaging, and fluid biomarker
The primary cognitive outcome was the change in the PACC score from baseline to week 240, with assessments conducted every 24 weeks. The PACC is a four‐component composite measure designed to capture amyloid‐related cognitive decline in preclinical AD.
37
It combines performance on the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE),
38
Logical Delayed Episodic Learning (LDEL),
39
,
40
Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST),
41
and Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test (FCSRT).
42
Three versions of the component subtests were alternated to minimize practice effects, and testing was conducted by trained site psychometrists blinded to treatment arm assignment and adverse events. Each component score was converted to a z‐score by subtracting the baseline mean and dividing by the baseline standard deviation for that component.
37
The PACC score was calculated as the average of the four z‐scores, with negative scores indicating cognitive decline.
The baseline clinical features utilized as predictors in the progression models included the PACC and its four components, Clinical Dementia Rating Scale–Sum of Boxes (CDR–SB),
43
and both participant and partner versions of the Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study Activities of Daily Living (ADCS‐ADL)
44
and the Cognitive Function Index (CFI).
45
All eligible participants underwent structural T1‐weighted MRI and 18F‐Florbetapir amyloid PET. The MRI scans were processed using FreeSurfer Version 7.3.1 to extract brain MRI measures, including cortical volume, surface area, and thickness, across multiple regions of interest according to the Desikan–Killiany atlas.
46
This process resulted in a total of 207 regional brain measures, with cortical thickness reported in millimeters (mm), volume in cubic millimeters (mm
3
), and area in square millimeters (mm
2
). All measures were normalized by intracranial volume to adjust for differences in head size. To enhance analysis efficiency and focus on relevant data, the baseline structural MRI features utilized by the prediction models were limited to the 18 structural brain network (SBN) modules and 36 hub regional measures identified in the recent publication.
29
This approach effectively reduced the redundancy and the number of prediction features.
Amyloid‐PET uptake was measured using mean cortical SUVR with a cerebellar reference, as previously described.
47
Amyloid status for A4 eligibility was assessed using an algorithm that combined quantitative SUVR methods with qualitative visual read performed at a central laboratory. SUVR values were converted to the standard Centiloid (CL) scale
48
and included as a baseline predictor in the prediction models.
Tau PET imaging with 18F‐flortaucipir (FTP) was available for a subset of 339 participants included in this analysis. The data were processed using the Stanford Tau PET Pipeline.
49
T1‐weighted MRI scans were processed with FreeSurfer to generate cortical segmentations. PET frames were realigned and summed, with additional smoothing (4 mm full width at half maximum [FWHM]) applied to noisy scans from a subset of participants. PET images were co‐registered to MRI using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM) software and regional mean uptake values were extracted from the FreeSurfer‐defined regions. Bilateral regions were computed via volume‐weighted averaging across hemispheres. All tau PET values were normalized to a gray matter‐only cerebellar cortex reference region. In the progression models, all bilateral regional SUVRs were included as predictors, derived from cortical and subcortical gray matter regions defined by the FreeSurfer Desikan–Killiany atlas. Regions were excluded if they exhibited off‐target binding (e.g., choroid plexus, brainstem), nonspecific signal (e.g., white matter), or were structurally irrelevant or used as the reference region (e.g., ventricles, cerebellum).
pTau217 concentrations were quantified on plasma samples using a validated electrochemiluminescence (ECL) immunoassay, performed on the Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) Sector S 600 MM platform at the CAP‐accredited, CLIA‐certified Lilly Clinical Diagnostics Laboratory.
50
2.3
Data analysis
2.3.1
Prediction model development and evaluation
Prognostic models were developed to predict individual‐level trajectories of PACC change over 4.5 years (240 weeks) using the Stochastic Gradient Boosting Machine (SGBM) algorithm.
51
Models incorporated various combinations of baseline predictors, including clinical assessments, global amyloid PET CL, plasma pTau217, MRI‐derived SBN modules and regional hubs, and bilateral SUVRs from cortical and subcortical gray matter regions on tau PET (excluding regions with potential off‐target binding or nonspecific signal). Tau PET regions exhibiting off‐target binding or nonspecific signal, such as the choroid plexus, brainstem, ventricles, white matter, and cerebellum (used as reference) were excluded. All models additionally included demographic variables (age, sex, and education), apolipoprotein E (
APOE
) ε4 allelic count, treatment arm, and PACC test version, with actual visit time (often delayed due to coronavirus disease 2019 [COVID‐19]) modeled as a continuous variable.
SGBM was selected for this study due to its demonstrated effectiveness in modeling cognitive decline in early AD
29
and clinical trial populations,
33
as well as its capacity to handle high‐dimensional data such as MRI, tau PET, and clinical assessments. SGBM is an ensemble learning method that aggregates predictions from multiple classification and regression trees (CART) to improve prediction accuracy. It can capture complex nonlinear effects and interactions among predictors without requiring explicit distributional assumptions or prespecified functional forms. Overfitting is controlled through internal tuning using cross‐validation. In this study, models were configured with up to 500 trees and allowed interactions up to the fifth order in base CART. Predictor importance was quantified based on the reduction in mean squared error (MSE) attributed to each variable, normalized to a 0%–100% scale.
52
Relationships between predictors and outcomes, including nonlinear effects and interactions, were explored using individual conditional expectation (ICE) profiles and partial dependence plots (PDP).
53
Unlike parametric regression models, which require prespecified model structures, the SGBM automatically identified influential predictors (e.g., pTau217, specific regional tau PET SUVRs) and enabled interpretable visualizations through ICE and PDP interaction plots.
Models were trained using both placebo and treatment arm participants, with treatment arm assignment included as a covariate. To evaluate prediction of natural cognitive decline, performance was assessed exclusively in held‐out placebo participants from the test folds. As illustrated in Figure
1
, we employed a repeated five‐fold cross‐validation framework: in each iteration, the dataset was randomly partitioned into five folds, with each fold held out once for testing and the remaining four used for training. This process was repeated 50 times to account for variability due to data partitioning and to ensure robust performance estimates. Within each iteration, predictions for placebo participants across the five test folds were aggregated and compared to observed PACC outcomes. Continuous predictions of PACC change were evaluated using robust Huber‐type R
2
,
54
Harrell's concordance index (C‐index),
55
and median absolute error (MAE). For binary classification of cognitive decline, thresholds of −0.25 and −0.5 in PACC change were applied, corresponding to declines of at least 0.25 points and 0.5 points, respectively. Classification performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). All reported performance metrics were calculated separately for each of the 50 cross‐validation iterations and summarized by their median values, providing stable and generalizable estimates of model performance. This repeated cross‐validation approach captures both individual variability in cognitive trajectories and uncertainty introduced by data partitioning.

2.3.2
Application of baseline‐predicted PACC decline as APC
A central application explored in this study is the use of baseline‐predicted individual cognitive trajectories, derived from prognostic models, as a time‐varying APC in the MMRM analysis. This approach aims to improve the precision of treatment effect estimates in clinical trials and, consequently, enhance statistical power and reduce required sample sizes when applied prospectively in trial design.
To evaluate this, we conducted a simulation study examining how time‐varying APCs with varying strengths of association with clinical outcomes influence MMRM performance. A total of 5000 synthetic clinical trial datasets were generated using a mixture of multivariate normal distributions, calibrated to approximately match the means, variances, correlation structures, and distribution skewness observed in the A4 Study. Simulated trials replicated real‐world missing data patterns, including dropout rates of 3%, 7%, 14%, 20%, and 30% at weeks 48, 96, 144, 192, and 240, respectively. Participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to placebo or active treatment arms, with each trial spanning 240 weeks.
Placebo group trajectories were simulated to align with observed PACC changes among participants with amyloid PET CL greater than 40 in the A4 Study. This threshold was applied in the simulation to define a preclinical AD population with sufficiently elevated amyloid levels. The treatment group was simulated to exhibit a slower rate of decline, reaching 62% of the placebo group's rate of decline by week 240. Additionally,
APOE
ε4 status and baseline PACC score were simulated to reflect their distributions in the A4 cohort and were included as covariates in the MMRM, along with treatment group and interaction terms for APC by time, and treatment by time. The APC used in the MMRM was constructed by fixing the treatment indicator at placebo in the prediction model trained via the SBGM algorithm. Time was modeled as a fixed categorical effect rather than a continuous variable.
Nine simulation scenarios were tested, each varying the predictive strength of the APC at week 240, quantified by a distinct
R
2
value ranging from 0 to 0.42. The temporal pattern of APC–outcome association was modeled to increase over time, consistent with trends observed in the A4 data. Two key outcomes were assessed: (1) the sample size required to detect a 38% slowing in cognitive decline at week 240 with 80% power and a two‐sided 5% Type I error rate, and (2) the statistical power to detect the same effect with a fixed sample size of 504 participants per arm. We assumed a 38% slowing of PACC decline for trial simulations, consistent with prevention–trial literature.
59
A 40%–50% slowing on PACC has been shown to delay progression from cognitively unimpaired to early mild cognitive impairment (MCI) by ∼3 years, a clinically meaningful difference. Our assumption of a 38% decline is closely aligned with this established range. This simulation framework enabled a rigorous evaluation of how incorporating APCs with varying predictive accuracy can enhance trial efficiency while maintaining statistical validity.
All analyses were performed using R Version 4.5 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing),
60
with specific packages gbm3,
61
pdp,
62
mmrm,
63
and emeans.

RESULTS
3.1
Study population and cognitive trajectories
The baseline characteristics of the 1023 participants in the A4 Study (519 placebo, 504 solanezumab) with complete amyloid PET, clinical, plasma pTau217, MRI, and longitudinal PACC data are presented in Table
1
. Participants had a mean age of 71.9 years (SD 4.8), with 58.5% female and 94.4% White. The average education level was 16.6 years (SD 2.7). Overall, 59.3% carried at least one
APOE
ε4 allele, with 51.0% heterozygous and 8.3% homozygous. Baseline amyloid burden, measured by amyloid PET CL, averaged 66.0 (SD 32.6), plasma pTau217 was 0.28 (SD 0.16), PACC was 0.05 (SD 2.7), and MMSE was 28.8 (SD 1.3). No significant differences were observed between placebo and solanezumab groups (
p
> 0.05), ensuring balanced cohorts for analysis.
Longitudinal PACC trajectories (Figure
2A
) revealed substantial heterogeneity in individual cognitive changes over the 240‐week study period, with some participants maintaining stable cognition and others showing varying rates of decline, particularly after week 192. This variability highlights the complexity of predicting continuous cognitive trajectories in preclinical AD. Mean PACC changes over time (Figure
2B
) indicated a gradual decline in both groups, with no significant treatment effect observed at week 240.
36
Progression models were trained using the full A4 dataset, with treatment arm and PACC version included as inputs.
3.2
Multimodal models for predicting cognitive decline
Prognostic models were developed to predict 4.5‐year (240‐week) PACC trajectories using SGBM, based on various combinations of baseline predictors (Table
S1
). In scenarios without tau PET data, Model‐1, which included clinical assessments (PACC and its four components, CDR‐SB, ADCS‐ADL, and CFI), plasma pTau217, and MRI‐derived structural brain network (SBN) modules and regional hubs, demonstrated robust performance for predicting continuous PACC change. At week 192, Model‐1 achieved a cross‐validated
R
2
of 0.395, C‐index of 0.688, and MAE of 0.361. Performance remained stable at week 216 (
R
2
= 0.329,
C
‐index = 0.655, MAE = 0.455) and week 240 (

2
= 0.324, C‐index = 0.663, MAE = 0.452). Adding global amyloid PET CL to Model‐1 yielded similar results (
R
2
= 0.386, 0.331, and 0.329 at weeks 192, 216, and 240, respectively), suggesting that clinical, plasma, and MRI features captured much of the prognostic signal without requiring amyloid PET.
When tau PET data were available (
n
= 339), Model‐2, which included baseline clinical assessments, plasma pTau217, and regional tau PET SUVRs, showed improved predictive performance. At week 192, Model‐2 achieved a cross‐validated
R
2
of 0.421, C‐index of 0.694, and MAE of 0.373. Performance remained strong at week 216 (R
2
= 0.463, C‐index = 0.675, MAE = 0.437) and week 240 (
R
2
= 0.419, C‐index = 0.668, MAE = 0.425). Adding MRI‐derived features to Model‐2 did not improve accuracy meaningfully (
R
2
= 0.430, 0.458, and 0.413 across timepoints), indicating that once tau PET is available, structural MRI adds limited incremental value. Full performance metrics for Models 1 and 2 are provided in Table

.
Both models also demonstrated strong binary classification performance for different PACC decline thresholds (0.25 and 0.5 points). At week 240, Model‐1 achieved AUROC values of 0.772 (0.25 threshold) and 0.786 (0.5 threshold), with the corresponding sensitivity/specificity of 0.57/0.85 and 0.46/0.93, respectively. Model‐2 showed higher discriminative accuracy: AUROC = 0.793 (0.25) and 0.831 (0.5), sensitivity = 0.58 and 0.54, specificity = 0.84 and 0.92. Differences in AUROC between the two models were statistically significant at week 240 (DeLong's test
p
< 0.05). Detailed classification performance for both models is shown in Table

, with additional metrics in Table

Figure
3
summarizes the most influential predictors and modeled effects for long‐term PACC decline. In Model‐1, plasma pTau217, MMSE, and PACC scores were the strongest contributors, followed by episodic memory components (LDEL, FCSRT) and MRI‐based SBNs (SBN‐15, SBN‐4, SBN‐18; Figure
3A
). These SBNs corresponded to regions associated with early AD pathology: entorhinal and temporal pole (SBN‐15), medial temporal lobe structures (SBN‐4), and lateral parietal and temporal areas involved in visuospatial integration (SBN‐18). In Model‐2, regional tau PET SUVRs dominated (Figure
3B
), with entorhinal, fusiform, amygdala, and inferior temporal cortex emerging as top predictors, alongside pTau217. Notably, the most influential tau PET regions were localized to medial temporal lobe (entorhinal) and early neocortical (fusiform, inferior temporal) regions, consistent with prior reports linking tau burden in these areas to future cognitive decline,
23
,
65
with over 75% of participants exhibiting tau SUVR > 1.1 in early neocortex (Table
1
). Age, PACC, and CFI (partner report) also contributed. These findings reinforce the dominant role of pTau217 across models and demonstrate the added value of tau PET for capturing early regional tau pathology. The diminished influence of MRI‐based SBNs in Model‐2 suggests that molecular imaging may offer more direct insights into AD‐related neurodegeneration than structural measures.
Nonlinear and interaction effects of key predictors were visualized using ICE and PDP analyses. Clustered ICE plots (Figure
3C–E
) showed that higher baseline plasma pTau217, entorhinal tau SUVR, and amygdala tau SUVR were each associated with greater PACC decline at week 240, while also revealing inter‐individual heterogeneity in the magnitude of these effects. Smoothed trajectories from generalized additive models revealed inflection points and nonlinear patterns, suggesting that the predictor effects are not uniformly expressed across individuals. Two‐dimensional partial dependence plots (Figure
3F–I
) revealed synergistic interactions. For example, individuals with both lower volume in SBN‐15 (entorhinal/temporal pole) and lower memory scores (PACC.FCTOTAL) at baseline experienced greater decline (Figure
3F
) at week 240. Co‐elevation of baseline tau in entorhinal and fusiform cortex (Figure
3G
), or high baseline pTau217 with elevated tau uptake in the amygdala (Figure
3H
) or inferior temporal cortex (Figure
3I
), predicted the more severe decline. Notably, when baseline pTau217 exceeded 0.3 and regional tau SUVR exceeded 1.4, the predicted PACC decline was substantially greater than with either marker alone, indicating non‐additive effects of these predictors.
Model predictions were further validated visually in Figure
4
. Mean predicted versus observed PACC trajectories (Figure
4A
) demonstrated that both models closely tracked longitudinal decline, with Model‐2 predictions (blue) aligning more closely with the observed group means at later time points. Subject‐level comparisons (Figure
4B
) showed good correlation between predicted and observed outcomes at weeks 192–240, with the majority falling within 95% prediction intervals. ROC curves (Figure
4C–D
) confirmed robust discrimination for both models at both decline thresholds, with consistently higher AUROC values for Model‐2, particularly at week 240.
To address scenarios where plasma pTau217 data are unavailable or derived from nonharmonized assays, we evaluated pTau217‐free models (Table
S1
). When tau PET was not available, Model‐3, which included baseline amyloid PET Centiloid, clinical assessments, and MRI‐derived measures, achieved
R
2
values of 0.265 at week 192, 0.261 at week 216, and 0.249 at week 240 (C‐index = 0.654, MAE = 0.484 at week 240). When tau PET was available, Model‐4, which combined global amyloid PET Centiloid, clinical assessments, and regional tau PET SUVRs, showed a substantially improved performance (

These models provide viable alternatives when plasma‐based biomarkers are unavailable or assay comparability is uncertain. Results for these and all other predictor combinations are presented in Table

.
We also evaluated a simplified model (Model‐5) that incorporated only clinical assessments and plasma pTau217, without MRI or tau PET (Table
S1
). At weeks 192, 216, and 240, Model‐5 achieved cross‐validated R
2
values of 0.341, 0.304, and 0.291, respectively, with corresponding C‐indices of 0.676, 0.663, and 0.650, and MAEs of 0.366, 0.472, and 0.451. While its accuracy was lower than that of Models 1, 2, and 4, which leveraged imaging data, the performance of Model‐5 was still robust enough to provide meaningful prognostic value. These findings suggest that a streamlined model based solely on baseline plasma biomarkers and cognitive measures could offer a practical alternative in settings where access to advanced imaging is limited, for example, in decentralized or “low‐touch” trial designs that prioritize logistical feasibility and reduced participant burden.
A sensitivity analysis using a single 50–50 train–test split of the combined placebo and treatment arms, stratified by treatment group, was conducted, with performance evaluated in held‐out placebo participants to align with our focus on natural cognitive decline. The analysis confirmed the relative rank order and performance trends of Models 1 through 5, with R
2
values generally within 0.06 points of those from repeated five‐fold cross‐validation. As single‐split evaluations can be sensitive to the specific data partition [refs.

Enhancing trial efficiency with prognostic covariates
To evaluate the impact of prognostic covariates on trial efficiency, we performed 5000 simulations using APCs designed to span a broad range of predictive strengths, with
R
2
values up to 0.42. These simulations replicated the data structure and dropout patterns observed in the A4 study, and assessed how incorporating APCs with varying predictive strengths influenced treatment effect estimation within the MMRM analyses.
As shown in Figure

and Table

R

2
= 0) yielded 80.2% power and, as expected, no reduction in variance. In contrast, incorporating Model‐1's APC reduced variance by 27%, increasing power to 90.6%, while Model‐2's APC achieved a 38.1% variance reduction, further improving power to 94.7%. These improvements scaled continuously with R
2

5B
illustrates the corresponding impact on sample size requirements when power is fixed at 80% and type I error at 5%. Model‐1's APC reduced the required sample size per group by 23.8% (from 504 to 384 participants), and Model‐2's APC achieved a 34.9% reduction (to 328 participants). Detailed results across a range of APC strengths are provided in Table
3
, which shows that even modestly predictive APCs (e.g., R
2
= 0.11) can increase power to 84.4% and reduce sample size needs by 8.7%.
Simulations also assessed the effect of APCs derived from Model‐3 and Model‐4 (Table

, Figure
5A–B
), which do not use baseline plasma pTau217. For a fixed sample size of 504 per group, Model‐3's APC (
R
2
= 0.249 at week 240) increased power to 87.8% and reduced variance by 19.5%, with a 17.3% sample size reduction (to 417 per group) for 80% power. Model‐4's APC (
R
2
= 0.387 at week 240) achieved 92.9% power, 33% variance reduction, and a 30% sample size reduction (to 353 per group). These results demonstrate that Model‐3 and Model‐4 can still offer substantial trial efficiency gains in settings where pTau217 data are unavailable or derived from different assays.
Simulations also incorporated the APC derived from Model‐5, which used only clinical assessments and plasma pTau217 (week 240

). While less efficient than models incorporating MRI or tau PET, these results demonstrate that Model‐5 can nonetheless yield meaningful trial efficiency gains.
Together, these findings demonstrate that incorporating APCs into trial analyses can substantially enhance statistical efficiency. Model‐1 offers practical benefits in settings where tau PET is unavailable, whereas Model‐2 yields greater gains when tau PET data are available, enabling more precise and powerful detection of treatment effects with fewer participants. Similarly, Model‐3 and Model‐4 are suitable options when pTau217 data are not available, and Model‐5 is a suitable option when imaging data (MRI and PET) are not available.
These reductions in sample size requirements also yield substantial benefits for participant screening, a major hurdle in preclinical AD trials. In the A4 study, 82.7% of screened individuals did not meet the inclusion criteria, implying that approximately six individuals had to be screened to enroll one participant.
36
Thus, the 23.8% reduction in sample size achieved with Model‐1's APC corresponds to approximately 1,387 fewer individuals needing to be screened. Similarly, the 34.9% reduction enabled by Model‐2's APC translates to 2034 fewer individuals requiring screening. These findings highlight the practical advantages of incorporating prognostic covariates, not only in improving statistical power and precision but also in significantly easing recruitment demands in preclinical AD trials.
4
DISCUSSION
This study developed and evaluated machine learning models to predict 4.5‐year cognitive decline trajectories, as measured by PACC change, in cognitively unimpaired, amyloid‐positive older adults from the A4 study. Using baseline multimodal data, including clinical assessments, plasma pTau217, structural MRI, and regional tau PET SUVRs, we first constructed two prognostic models: Model‐1 (clinical, pTau217, MRI) and Model‐2 (clinical, pTau217, tau PET). Both models demonstrated strong predictive performance, with Model‐1 offering a practical solution for settings without tau PET and Model‐2 providing enhanced accuracy when tau PET is available. The baseline predictions of future PACC decline trajectories from these models, when used as time‐varying APCs in the MMRM analyses for treatment effect assessments, substantially improved trial efficiency by increasing power and reducing sample sizes. Our findings advance preclinical AD research by enabling forecasting of cognitive decline and optimization of trial design.
The robust performance of Model‐1 (
R
2
= 0.324, AUROC = 0.786 for classifying a 0.5‐point PACC decline at week 240) highlights the value of combining plasma pTau217, clinical assessments, and MRI‐derived measures to predict cognitive decline. Plasma pTau217 was a key predictor (Figure
3A
), consistent with prior studies highlighting its prognostic utility in preclinical AD.
17
,
18
The model's reliance on MRI‐derived cortical thickness and volume in medial and lateral temporal as well as parietal regions, including the entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, fusiform gyrus, temporal pole, precuneus, and inferior parietal lobule, aligns with well‐established patterns of early structural degeneration in AD.
27
,
28
,
29
Model‐1's performance makes it particularly valuable in clinical settings where tau PET imaging is not feasible due to cost or availability, offering a practical alternative in clinical studies for trial enrichment, patient stratification, or APC application.
Model‐2, which incorporated regional tau PET SUVRs, achieved superior predictive performance, particularly at later time points when cognitive decline was more pronounced (e.g.,
R
2
= 0.419 and AUROC = 0.831 for classifying a 0.5‐point PACC decline at week 240; Table
2
). The prominence of entorhinal, fusiform, inferior temporal, and amygdala tau SUVRs (Figure
3B
) underscores the critical role of early regional tau pathology, consistent with prior reports that tau burden in some medial temporal and early neocortical areas predicts future cognitive decline in preclinical AD.
23
,
65
The ICE profiles (Figure
3C–E
) revealed nonlinear and heterogeneous effects of baseline pTau217 and regional tau PET on 4.5‐year decline. Two‐way partial dependence plots (Figure
3F–I
) further demonstrated synergistic interactions between plasma pTau217 and PET‐derived tau measures, where concurrent elevations across both modalities, and in multiple early tau‐accumulation regions, were associated with substantially greater cognitive decline than either marker alone. These findings highlight the mechanistic importance of tau accumulation in preclinical AD and demonstrate the added prognostic value of tau PET when available.
The use of SGBM contributed to the robust performance of these prediction models by capturing nonlinear relationships and interactions. SGBM has previously demonstrated success in forecasting AD progression trajectories in early‐stage populations
29
and, more importantly, in improving the precision and power of treatment effect assessments in clinical trials,
33
supporting its consideration in predictive modeling and its application in preclinical AD trials.
The application of model‐derived predictions as APCs in the MMRM analyses significantly improved trial efficiency (Table
3
, Figure
5
). Model‐1's APC (
R
2
= 0.32) increased power from 80% to 90.6% and reduced sample size by 23.8% for detecting a 38% treatment effect at week 240. Model‐2's APC (
R
2
= 0.42) further improved power to 94.7% and reduced sample size by 34.9%. These gains are particularly impactful in preclinical AD trials, where subtle cognitive changes and high variability necessitate large cohorts to achieve adequate power.
7
,
8
,
9
,
35
Moreover, since 82.7% of screened individuals in A4 did not meet inclusion criteria,
36
these reductions translate to screening approximately 1387 and 2034 fewer individuals when applying APCs derived from Models 1 and 2, respectively. By adjusting for inter‐individual progression variability, APCs enhance the precision of treatment effect estimates, as seen in prior applications in early AD trials.
33
Our results extend this approach to the preclinical stage, offering a scalable strategy to design more efficient trials while mitigating recruitment burden without the need for more restrictive inclusion criteria.
The pronounced heterogeneity in individual PACC trajectories (Figure
2A
) highlights the challenge of predicting cognitive decline in preclinical AD. Detecting meaningful treatment effects requires prognostic tools capable of disentangling natural variability from true therapeutic impact, improving sensitivity and interpretability in trial analyses. Both models captured this variability, with Model‐2's superior performance at later time points aligning with emerging decline (Table
2
). The binary classification metrics (Table
S2
) further demonstrated the models’ ability to identify cognitive decline at different thresholds, with Model‐2 achieving higher accuracy. These results highlight the models’ potential to capture individual variability in preclinical AD, supporting broader applications in clinical trial design and beyond.
Beyond use as APCs, the prognostic models offer additional applications in clinical trials and potential real‐world settings. The models’ ability to identify individuals at higher risk of cognitive decline (Figure
4C–D
) supports clinical trial enrichment, enabling the selection of participants likely to progress, which can enhance trial sensitivity.
29
,
66
However, enrichment should be applied cautiously and only when clear evidence suggests that the treatment is more effective for a specific subpopulation, such as individuals with mild to moderate cognitive decline, to avoid unnecessarily excluding those who might benefit. The APC approach is particularly valuable when there is no justification for excluding subgroups, as it improves trial efficiency without imposing restrictive inclusion criteria to enhance patient population homogeneity. Other trial applications include precision medicine investigations through patient stratification, subgroup identification of treatment responders versus nonresponders, and targeted post hoc evaluations of treatment effects and adverse events, which could inform personalized trial designs. In real‐world settings, these predictions could serve as a supplemental tool for patient diagnosis, monitoring, and decisions related to treatment and care.
67
,
68
This would require assessments feasible in routine practice, such as simpler cognitive tests or widely available biomarkers, and potentially higher accuracy than achieved by Model‐1 and Model‐2 to guide therapeutic decisions.
In settings where imaging is unavailable or operationally constrained, Model‐5 offers a more streamlined alternative. Using only clinical assessments and plasma pTau217, it achieved reasonable predictive accuracy (
R
2
= 0.291; AUROC = 0.783 for 0.5‐point PACC decline). When used to derived APC, Model‐5improved trial power to 89.1% with a 20.8% reduction in sample size. These findings support its use in decentralized or low‐touch trial designs, where access to MRI or PET may be limited.
Despite these strengths, our study has limitations. First, the A4 study population was predominantly White (94.4%), which limits the generalizability of the models to more racially and ethnically diverse populations. Participants were also highly educated, which may reduce applicability to groups with lower educational attainment, where differences in cognitive reserve and cultural factors could influence cognitive trajectories. Future studies should aim to expand and validate these models in cohorts with greater racial, ethnic, and educational diversity.
Another limitation is the lack of standardization across plasma pTau217 assays, a key predictor in Models 1, 2, and 5. These models were developed using data from an MSD ECL immunoassay,
50
which may limit their applicability to studies using other assay platforms unless harmonization protocols are implemented. Future standardization and bridging efforts will enhance model generalizability across different pTau217 assays. In settings where assay comparability is a concern, alternative models that exclude pTau217 (Models 3 and 4) offer viable options (see Results
3.2
,
3.3
).
Finally, tau PET data were available for only a subset of participants (
n
= 339), reducing the sample size for Model‐2 development. In contrast, Model‐1 was trained and evaluated on the full cohort (
n
= 1,023) and demonstrated consistent performance. Larger tau PET datasets could further improve the predictive performance and robustness of Model‐2.
Future work should address some priorities to advance the application of these models in preclinical AD. First, validation in independent cohorts is essential to confirm generalizability. Second, because Model‐2's performance improved with regional tau PET SUVRs (Model‐2), incorporating tau‐specific fluid biomarkers such as CSF or plasma MTBR‐tau243,
69
,
70
may provide comparable accuracy while avoiding the need for tau PET. Third, building on recent evidence demonstrating the impact of APCs for improving the precision and power of treatment effect assessments in early AD trials, a logical next step is to apply this approach in preclinical AD trials using the proposed APC. This includes prospectively incorporating APCs into trial designs to evaluate their practical utility in enhancing statistical power and reducing required sample sizes. Finally, developing user‐friendly tools to implement APCs in trial analyses could facilitate broader adoption in clinical studies, ultimately supporting more efficient evaluation of preventive therapies in preclinical AD.
In conclusion, multimodal prognostic models can predict cognitive decline trajectories in preclinical AD at baseline, with Model‐1 providing a practical solution in the absence of tau PET and Model‐2 achieving greater accuracy when tau PET is available. By incorporating these predictions as APCs, clinical trials can achieve significant efficiency gains, reducing sample size requirements and increasing statistical power. These findings pave the way for more effective trial designs and lay the groundwork for future personalized strategies in preclinical AD, ultimately supporting the development of therapies to delay or prevent cognitive decline.
