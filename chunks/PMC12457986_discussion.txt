DISCUSSIONPrognostic models can predict decline in preclinical AD and improve trial efficiency. 4DISCUSSIONThis study developed and evaluated machine learning models to predict 4.5‐year cognitive decline trajectories, as measured by PACC change, in cognitively unimpaired, amyloid‐positive older adults from the A4 study. Using baseline multimodal data, including clinical assessments, plasma pTau217, structural MRI, and regional tau PET SUVRs, we first constructed two prognostic models: Model‐1 (clinical, pTau217, MRI) and Model‐2 (clinical, pTau217, tau PET). Both models demonstrated strong predictive performance, with Model‐1 offering a practical solution for settings without tau PET and Model‐2 providing enhanced accuracy when tau PET is available. The baseline predictions of future PACC decline trajectories from these models, when used as time‐varying APCs in the MMRM analyses for treatment effect assessments, substantially improved trial efficiency by increasing power and reducing sample sizes. Our findings advance preclinical AD research by enabling forecasting of cognitive decline and optimization of trial design.The robust performance of Model‐1 (R 2 = 0.324, AUROC = 0.786 for classifying a 0.5‐point PACC decline at week 240) highlights the value of combining plasma pTau217, clinical assessments, and MRI‐derived measures to predict cognitive decline. Plasma pTau217 was a key predictor (Figure 3A), consistent with prior studies highlighting its prognostic utility in preclinical AD. 17 , 18 The model's reliance on MRI‐derived cortical thickness and volume in medial and lateral temporal as well as parietal regions, including the entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, fusiform gyrus, temporal pole, precuneus, and inferior parietal lobule, aligns with well‐established patterns of early structural degeneration in AD. 27 , 28 , 29 Model‐1's performance makes it particularly valuable in clinical settings where tau PET imaging is not feasible due to cost or availability, offering a practical alternative in clinical studies for trial enrichment, patient stratification, or APC application.Model‐2, which incorporated regional tau PET SUVRs, achieved superior predictive performance, particularly at later time points when cognitive decline was more pronounced (e.g., R 2 = 0.419 and AUROC = 0.831 for classifying a 0.5‐point PACC decline at week 240; Table 2). The prominence of entorhinal, fusiform, inferior temporal, and amygdala tau SUVRs (Figure 3B) underscores the critical role of early regional tau pathology, consistent with prior reports that tau burden in some medial temporal and early neocortical areas predicts future cognitive decline in preclinical AD. 23 , 65 The ICE profiles (Figure 3C–E) revealed nonlinear and heterogeneous effects of baseline pTau217 and regional tau PET on 4.5‐year decline. Two‐way partial dependence plots (Figure 3F–I) further demonstrated synergistic interactions between plasma pTau217 and PET‐derived tau measures, where concurrent elevations across both modalities, and in multiple early tau‐accumulation regions, were associated with substantially greater cognitive decline than either marker alone. These findings highlight the mechanistic importance of tau accumulation in preclinical AD and demonstrate the added prognostic value of tau PET when available.The use of SGBM contributed to the robust performance of these prediction models by capturing nonlinear relationships and interactions. SGBM has previously demonstrated success in forecasting AD progression trajectories in early‐stage populations 29 and, more importantly, in improving the precision and power of treatment effect assessments in clinical trials, 33 supporting its consideration in predictive modeling and its application in preclinical AD trials.The application of model‐derived predictions as APCs in the MMRM analyses significantly improved trial efficiency (Table 3, Figure 5). Model‐1's APC (R 2 = 0.32) increased power from 80% to 90.6% and reduced sample size by 23.8% for detecting a 38% treatment effect at week 240. Model‐2's APC (R 2 = 0.42) further improved power to 94.7% and reduced sample size by 34.9%. These gains are particularly impactful in preclinical AD trials, where subtle cognitive changes and high variability necessitate large cohorts to achieve adequate power. 7 , 8 , 9 , 35 Moreover, since 82.7% of screened individuals in A4 did not meet inclusion criteria, 36 these reductions translate to screening approximately 1387 and 2034 fewer individuals when applying APCs derived from Models 1 and 2, respectively. By adjusting for inter‐individual progression variability, APCs enhance the precision of treatment effect estimates, as seen in prior applications in early AD trials. 33 Our results extend this approach to the preclinical stage, offering a scalable strategy to design more efficient trials while mitigating recruitment burden without the need for more restrictive inclusion criteria.The pronounced heterogeneity in individual PACC trajectories (Figure 2A) highlights the challenge of predicting cognitive decline in preclinical AD. Detecting meaningful treatment effects requires prognostic tools capable of disentangling natural variability from true therapeutic impact, improving sensitivity and interpretability in trial analyses. Both models captured this variability, with Model‐2's superior performance at later time points aligning with emerging decline (Table 2). The binary classification metrics (Table S2) further demonstrated the models’ ability to identify cognitive decline at different thresholds, with Model‐2 achieving higher accuracy. These results highlight the models’ potential to capture individual variability in preclinical AD, supporting broader applications in clinical trial design and beyond.Beyond use as APCs, the prognostic models offer additional applications in clinical trials and potential real‐world settings. The models’ ability to identify individuals at higher risk of cognitive decline (Figure 4C–D) supports clinical trial enrichment, enabling the selection of participants likely to progress, which can enhance trial sensitivity. 29 , 66 However, enrichment should be applied cautiously and only when clear evidence suggests that the treatment is more effective for a specific subpopulation, such as individuals with mild to moderate cognitive decline, to avoid unnecessarily excluding those who might benefit. The APC approach is particularly valuable when there is no justification for excluding subgroups, as it improves trial efficiency without imposing restrictive inclusion criteria to enhance patient population homogeneity. Other trial applications include precision medicine investigations through patient stratification, subgroup identification of treatment responders versus nonresponders, and targeted post hoc evaluations of treatment effects and adverse events, which could inform personalized trial designs. In real‐world settings, these predictions could serve as a supplemental tool for patient diagnosis, monitoring, and decisions related to treatment and care. 67 , 68 This would require assessments feasible in routine practice, such as simpler cognitive tests or widely available biomarkers, and potentially higher accuracy than achieved by Model‐1 and Model‐2 to guide therapeutic decisions.In settings where imaging is unavailable or operationally constrained, Model‐5 offers a more streamlined alternative. Using only clinical assessments and plasma pTau217, it achieved reasonable predictive accuracy (R 2 = 0.291; AUROC = 0.783 for 0.5‐point PACC decline). When used to derived APC, Model‐5improved trial power to 89.1% with a 20.8% reduction in sample size. These findings support its use in decentralized or low‐touch trial designs, where access to MRI or PET may be limited.Despite these strengths, our study has limitations. First, the A4 study population was predominantly White (94.4%), which limits the generalizability of the models to more racially and ethnically diverse populations. Participants were also highly educated, which may reduce applicability to groups with lower educational attainment, where differences in cognitive reserve and cultural factors could influence cognitive trajectories. Future studies should aim to expand and validate these models in cohorts with greater racial, ethnic, and educational diversity.Another limitation is the lack of standardization across plasma pTau217 assays, a key predictor in Models 1, 2, and 5. These models were developed using data from an MSD ECL immunoassay, 50 which may limit their applicability to studies using other assay platforms unless harmonization protocols are implemented. Future standardization and bridging efforts will enhance model generalizability across different pTau217 assays. In settings where assay comparability is a concern, alternative models that exclude pTau217 (Models 3 and 4) offer viable options (see Results 3.2, 3.3).Finally, tau PET data were available for only a subset of participants (n = 339), reducing the sample size for Model‐2 development. In contrast, Model‐1 was trained and evaluated on the full cohort (n = 1,023) and demonstrated consistent performance. Larger tau PET datasets could further improve the predictive performance and robustness of Model‐2.Future work should address some priorities to advance the application of these models in preclinical AD. First, validation in independent cohorts is essential to confirm generalizability. Second, because Model‐2's performance improved with regional tau PET SUVRs (Model‐2), incorporating tau‐specific fluid biomarkers such as CSF or plasma MTBR‐tau243, 69 , 70 may provide comparable accuracy while avoiding the need for tau PET. Third, building on recent evidence demonstrating the impact of APCs for improving the precision and power of treatment effect assessments in early AD trials, a logical next step is to apply this approach in preclinical AD trials using the proposed APC. This includes prospectively incorporating APCs into trial designs to evaluate their practical utility in enhancing statistical power and reducing required sample sizes. Finally, developing user‐friendly tools to implement APCs in trial analyses could facilitate broader adoption in clinical studies, ultimately supporting more efficient evaluation of preventive therapies in preclinical AD.In conclusion, multimodal prognostic models can predict cognitive decline trajectories in preclinical AD at baseline, with Model‐1 providing a practical solution in the absence of tau PET and Model‐2 achieving greater accuracy when tau PET is available. By incorporating these predictions as APCs, clinical trials can achieve significant efficiency gains, reducing sample size requirements and increasing statistical power. These findings pave the way for more effective trial designs and lay the groundwork for future personalized strategies in preclinical AD, ultimately supporting the development of therapies to delay or prevent cognitive decline.
