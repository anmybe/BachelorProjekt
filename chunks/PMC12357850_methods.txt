Methods and proceduresData sourceAll data used in this study were extracted from the NHANES database, which is publicly available at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/about_nhanes.htm. To explore the relationship between liver stiffness and fat values measured by transient elastography and CHD, we extracted demographic information, physical examination data, laboratory results, and survey responses from 27,493 participants in the NHANES database from 2017–2020 (pre-pandemic) and 2021–2023. Participants with the following characteristics were excluded: (1) Missing liver stiffness values (n = 11,093); (2) Missing liver fat values (n = 3); (3) Missing CHD outcome data (n = 2902); (4) Missing educational attainment data (n = 8); (5) Missing antibody data (n = 799). Population weights were applied using NHANES-recommended sampling weights to generate nationally representative estimates.Due to the retrospective nature of this study and the absence of personally identifiable sensitive data in the publicly available database, ethical approval was waived for this research.Definition of CHDCHD diagnosis was determined based on responses to a medical history questionnaire conducted during individual interviews. Participants were classified as having CHD if they responded affirmatively to the question: “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had CHD?”.Data collectionDemographic and questionnaire data from the NHANES database were used to collect variables such as sex, age, race, education level, and income. Race was categorized as Non-Hispanic White, Mexican American, Non-Hispanic Black, Other Hispanic, or Other Races. Education level was classified as Less Than 9th Grade, 9–11th Grade, High School Graduate, Some College or Associate Degree, and College Graduate or Above. Antibody status was grouped into Indeterminate, Negative, or Positive. Laboratory and measurement modules were used to extract variables including BMI, hepatitis B surface antibody, stiffness (liver stiffness), controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) (liver fat values), and cholesterol (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flowchart of sample selection.Statistical methodsStatistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.0). Normality was assessed using the K-S test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) and analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Non-normally distributed variables were reported as medians (interquartile ranges) and analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons were performed using the chi-squared test.A logistic regression model was constructed to evaluate the causal relationship between the exposure variables (liver stiffness [stiffness] and liver fat values [CAP]) and the outcome variable (CHD incidence). This model also aimed to determine whether the exposure factors act as risk factors or protective factors. Since stiffness and CAP are continuous variables, they were categorized into quartiles: Q1 (lowest 25%), Q2 (25–50%), Q3 (50–75%), and Q4 (highest 25%). The model was constructed by directly performing logistic regression analysis on the exposure and outcome factors. Q1 served as the reference group, and the effects of Q2, Q3, and Q4 were compared against Q1.Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was used to examine potential nonlinear relationships between liver stiffness, CAP, and CHD risk. Subgroup analyses were conducted to validate the associations between stiffness, CAP, and CHD risk in different populations (e.g., by sex and race). To further assess the predictive value of different thresholds, we performed sensitivity analyses using various cut-off points and combinations for liver stiffness and CAP, calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and AUC for each. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value < 0.05.Machine learning model development and evaluationData preprocessing was performed as an initial step. Continuous variables were summarized using mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were ordinally encoded. For the binary classification target variable, One-Hot Encoding was applied to transform the data into a binary format, where 1 represented the presence of the disease and 0 represented its absence. In total, 10 features and 1 target variable were processed. To address the severe imbalance in the target variable within the original dataset, undersampling was applied to the majority class, reducing its sample size to achieve class balance. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the relationships between 8 continuous features and the target variable, with a coefficient of 1 indicating a perfect positive correlation, -1 indicating a perfect negative correlation, and 0 indicating no linear relationship.The preprocessed data were split into training and testing datasets using the sklearn.model_selection module in Python, ensuring that the distribution of the target variable categories was maintained. A total of 30% of the data was reserved as an independent test set to evaluate the reliability of model predictions and selection, while the remaining 70% was used as the training set for feature selection, model training, and hyperparameter tuning. To ensure effective model training, feature data were normalized using the MinMaxScaler, and five-fold cross-validation was performed on the training set to evaluate each model’s performance and optimize its hyperparameters. In the logistic regression model, hyperparameter C was optimized through grid search, with the optimal value determined to be C = 0.1, achieving a cross-validation score of 0.80. For the decision tree classifier, grid search optimization yielded optimal parameters of max_depth = 3 and min_samples_split = 2, resulting in a cross-validation score of 0.77. For the XGBoost classifier, the best parameters were identified as learning_rate = 0.1, max_depth = 3, and n_estimators = 50, with a cross-validation score of 0.80. For the neural network model, a network was constructed using the Keras framework, and grid search optimization determined the optimal parameters as batch_size = 20 and epochs = 20, achieving a cross-validation score of 0.73. Finally, the performance of all four models was evaluated on the test set using metrics such as the confusion matrix, accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and AUC-ROC. These metrics were used to comprehensively assess the predictive capabilities of the models and identify the most suitable model for CHD risk prediction.
