ResultsStudy participantsThe Phase 1 study of ProC6C was conducted in the Sabou health district in Burkina Faso with the full study details, primary objectives and study protocol published.18 All volunteers received three vaccinations four weeks apart. The present analysis included participants (n = 20 each group) from the 100 µg ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M (G2D) and the Hepatitis B (HepB, G2E) control groups alone as prior analysis indicated G2D was the preferred formulation to move forward in clinical development. The demographics of G2D and G2E are reported in Table 1. All samples (n = 20) were available from the ProC6C vaccine group (G2D, Table 1). Due to loss to follow-up (n = 3) or withdrawal of consent (n = 1) 16 of 20 samples (Table 1) were available from the control group (G2E) at D180. Blood samples were taken at baseline (D0), two weeks after each vaccination (D14, D42, D70) and at D140 and D180 to measure durability up to four months post-last dose. Plasmodium falciparum parasite presence was monitored throughout the study and reported (D0, D70, D180) in Table 1. In previous analysis, there was no observed impact on parasite impact and immunogenicity of ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M.18 All study participants were treated for malaria according to the study protocol and national guidelines.18Dynamics of antibody responses to ProC6C constituent antigensTo assess the evolution of IgG antibodies against the sexual stage antigen domains of ProC6C, specific IgG was quantitated in sera from volunteers receiving 100 µg ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M (G2D) or the Hepatitis B (G2E) control vaccines. IgG responses against the Pfs48/45-6C and Pfs230-Pro domains of ProC6C were determined at D0, D14, D42, D70, D140 and D180 and calculated using reference antibodies described. All volunteers receiving ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M generated Pfs48/45-6C- and Pfs230-Pro-specific IgG responses after a single administration and these responses were boosted by subsequent vaccinations with peak antibody titers at D70 (Figure 1a,c). At baseline (D0), geometric mean titer (GMT) in the vaccine and control groups were at similar levels, with 0.2 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.1 to 0.3) and 0.3 (0.2 to 0.4) for Pfs48/45-6C, and 10.1 (6.5 to 15.6) and 7.7 (5.1 to 11.7) for Pfs230-Pro, respectively (Supplementary Table S1). At D70, antigen-specific GMT in the vaccine group increased to 12.5 (9.2 to 17.1) and 49.4 (39.9 to 61.2) for Pfs48/45-6C and Pfs230-Pro, respectively (Supplementary Table S1) and the ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M group (G2D) showed significantly higher titers than the control group for both antigens (p < 0.0001 for Pfs48/45-6C and p < .0001 for Pfs230-Pro by unpaired t test). As expected, antibody titers decreased over time after D70 for both constituent antigens (Supplementary Table S1). At D180, Pfs48/45-6C GMT was 4.5 (3.1 to 6.6) and Pfs230-Pro GMT was 21.6 (15.7 to 29.8) in the vaccine group (G2D). In comparison, the GMTs in the control group (G2E) were 0.4 (0.3 to 0.5) for Pfs48/45-6C and 7.8 (4.7 to 13.1) for Pfs230-Pro. Thus, ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M vaccinated individuals (G2D) maintained significantly higher titers than those vaccinated with HepB (G2E) four months after the third vaccination (p < .0001 for Pfs48/45-6C and p = .0014 for Pfs230-Pro). GMT is indicated in supplementary table S1.Functional activity of paired D70 and D180 seraHaving shown that serum concentration of anti-Pfs48/45-6C and anti-Pfs230-Pro IgG antibodies remain present four month after the last vaccination (D180), it was of interest to determine whether these IgG antibodies were functional. IgG was purified from available paired D70 and D180 sera in the ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M (n = 20) and Hep B control (n = 16) groups, respectively and tested at physiological antibody concentrations (15 mg/mL) in the SMFA. Each individual data point represents percentage inhibition in oocyst density (TRA). Significant (p < .05) TRA is denoted by a closed circle and non-significant TRA by an open circle (Figure 2a, and Supplementary Table S2). Eighteen of twenty samples from the ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M group (G2D) had a significant (p < .05) TRA at D70 while only seven of these remained significant at D180 (Figure 2a, and Supplementary Table S2). For comparison, we also tested samples (n = 16) from the HepB group (G2E), which were available at both time points. The level of TRA in the ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M group was significantly higher than that in the control group (p = .0002) at D70, while there was no significant difference in TRA between these two groups (p = .513) at D180 suggesting that vaccine-induced functional immunity decline over time. Five D70 samples and three D180 samples from HepB-vaccinated individuals (n = 16) had a significant (p < .05) TRA (Figure 2a & Supplementary Table S2). The anti-Pfs230-Pro IgG antibody titers did not differ significantly between TRA positive and negative samples (Figure 2e), supporting that anti-Pfs48/45-6C IgG is the main contributor to TRA.Functional activity relative to antibodies to ProC6C constituent antigensTo further investigate the relationship between antibody titers and functional activity, TRA was plotted against the anti-Pfs48/45-6C or the anti-Pfs230-Pro IgG concentration for all samples. For ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M immunized individuals, levels of anti-Pfs48/45-6C IgG were strongly correlated with TRA (p < .0001, Spearman’s coefficient r = 0.7199) (Figure 3a). In contrast, anti-Pfs230-Pro antibodies were not correlated with TRA (p = .4282, Spearman’s coefficient r = 0.1288) in these individuals (Figure 3b), confirming the role of anti-Pfs48/45-6C IgG in TRA. There was no significant correlation between TRA and the concentration of Pfs48/45-6C IgG (Figure 3c; p = .9666) or Pfs230-Pro IgG (Figure 3d; p = .3990) in HepB vaccinated individuals. The p value and correlation coefficient (r) in each panel are shown.Functional activity in relation to specific antibody measurementWe focused the analysis against antibody measurements (biomarkers) against Pfs48/45-6C and specifically those against epitope I as this is a major target of TB antibodies including mAb TB31F.30 Specific antibodies against epitope I were determined by a competition ELISA where serial dilutions of serum samples from individuals receiving ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M (G2D) were incubated with recombinant Pfs48/45-6C and subsequently added to plates coated with TB31F. Antibody concentration was expressed as mAb equivalence units (µg/ml) calculated relative to the Pfs48/45-6C specific mAb TB31F. The level of epitope I-specific antibodies correlated (p < .0001, Spearman’s coefficient r = 0.7557) with the Pfs48/45-6C IgG concentrations (Figure 4). However, some samples showed a high signal in the Pfs48/45-6C ELISA and a low signal in the epitope I competition ELISA suggesting that the Pfs48/45-6C domain contain multiple epitopes for human antibodies. The red dotted line shows a y=x.To investigate the ability of anti-Pfs48/45 antibodies to discriminate between samples with and without significant TRA, empirical ROC curves were constructed using both anti-Pfs48/45-6C and anti-epitope I IgG. When all D70 and D180 samples from individuals immunized with ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M were included in the analysis, we found ROC area under the curve (AUCs) of 0.8933 (CI, 0.7954–0.9913; p < .0001) and 0.8267 (CI, 0.6996–0.9536; p < .0001), respectively. Considering a ROC AUC of greater than 0.8 as excellent,28 both antibody measures had excellent predictive accuracies (Figure 5a,b). The estimated threshold for significant TRA was 8.7 and 5.5 µg/ml for the anti-Pfs48/45-6C and anti-epitope I measurements, respectively using Youden’s topleft analysis. The performance characteristic of the threshold for anti-Pfs48.45-6C included a sensitivity of 0.933 (CI, 0.681–0.998) and specificity of 0.720 (CI, 0.506–0.879) while the anti-epitope I included a sensitivity of 0.867 (CI, 0.595–0.983) and specificity of 0.680 (CI, 0.465–0.851). A similar ROC curve analysis was conducted for anti-Pfs230-Pro (Supplementary Figure S1), however was found to be not significant (AUC of 0.628, p = .222).
