ResultsThe plasma concentration of NfL and GFAP was significantly higher in patients with RRMS than in HCs. For patients with NMOSD, only GFAP increased. The density of EVs assessed by NTA was lower in plasma of patients with RRMS than in HC plasma. In addition, the 3 disease groups presented increased mean EV sizes in comparison with HCs. Tetraspanin-based EV analyses by SP-IRIS allowed us to observe a modest difference in the level of CD81 in RRMS EVs. In patients with RRMS, but not in those with NMOSD and MOGAD, multiplex/flow cytometry analyses revealed changes in the EV levels of CD29, CD31, and CD69 associated with the time elapsed since the last relapse. The negative correlations established between the vesicular levels of CD31, CD40, CD44, CD49c, CD69, and NfL or GFAP z-scores suggest a negative relationship specifically in RRMS. ResultsPatient DemographicsThis study included patients diagnosed with RRMS (n = 52), AQP4-IgG seropositive NMOSD (n = 19), and MOG-IgG seropositive MOGAD (n = 10) and healthy donors (HC, n = 15) (Table). Patients with NMOSD were significantly older than those with RRMS and consisted of women only. In comparison, the RRMS and MOGAD groups comprised approximately two-thirds of women. The age at disease onset is in general later in the NMOSD group than in MS and MOGAD groups, and the predominance in women is stronger in the NMOSD group than in MS and MOGAD groups.24 Thus, the heterogeneity of the cohort is inherent to the diseases. Patients with NMOSD also exhibited more severe symptoms than patients with RRMS and MOGAD, as determined using the EDSS. At the time of blood/plasma collection, only a few patients experienced a clinical relapse. Most of the patients with RRMS was treated with natalizumab (NTZ), whereas patients with NMOSD received rituximab (RTX) and patients with MOGAD were given heterogeneous treatments (Table). The time elapsed since initiation of current treatment did not differ significantly between patient groups (data not shown). EV = extracellular vesicle.Isolated EV Characterization by NTAIn a more classical approach, EVs were isolated by size-exclusion chromatography before EV marker detection by Western blotting (Figure 3A), cryo-EM observation (Figure 3B), and NTA. The relatively low level of albumin found in EV samples in comparison with plasma suggests that most of the plasma proteins are eliminated in the isolation process (Figure 3A). Accordingly, enrichment in intravesicular protein Alix was observed in the EV lysate compared with plasma. Cryo-EM images of isolated EVs allowed us to distinguish typical lipid bilayer membranes in small vesicles (Figure 3B), thus confirming the preservation of EV integrity and morphology.We then sought to determine the size and concentrations of isolated EVs by NTA. NTA is commonly used to determine the density and size of EVs.26 Strikingly, NTA measurement revealed lower concentrations of EVs in the RRMS group than in HCs (Figure 4B). The mean EV size did not vary between the 3 disease groups (123.1 nm in RRMS; 114.3 nm in NMOSD, and 117.2 nm in MOGAD) but was significantly bigger than that of HCs (96.3 nm) (Figure 4C). Detailed analysis of EV size distribution showed a peak at 76.5 nm in patients with RRMS, 77.5 nm in patients with NMOSD, 76.5 nm in patients with MOGAD, and 72.5 nm in HCs, suggesting that small EVs represent the bulk of isolated vesicles (Figure 4D), consistent with cryo-EM observations. The distribution of EV sizes normalized to the total number of EVs did not reveal significant variations between disease groups (Figure 4D). Potential treatment effects on EV size and density were examined by comparing subgroups of patients with RRMS receiving NTZ (n = 33), dimethylfumarate/glatiramer acetate (n = 12), or no treatment (NT = 7) (eFigure 3). No significant differences were observed. EV subpopulations were isolated with capture beads coated with antibodies directed against 37 distinct EV epitopes (Figure 5A). The capture beads were discriminated according to PE and FITC fluorescence intensities (Figure 5B), and captured EVs were detected by the APC signal from the CD9, CD81, and CD63 antibodies. The APC intensity of each bead population was quantified. To different extents, the levels of CD29, CD31, CD40, CD44, and CD69, which are T-cell–associated and leukocyte-associated markers, significantly changed in patients with RRMS along with the time elapsed since the last relapse and NfL or GFAP z-scores (Figure 5C). CD29, CD31, and CD69 were positively associated with the time elapsed since the last relapse. A negative correlation was established between the levels of CD31, CD40, CD44, CD49c, and CD69 and plasma NfL in the RRMS sample in contrast to NMOSD, MOGAD, and HC samples, suggesting that increased expression of specific EV markers may be associated with low disease activity in RRMS (Figure 5C). A similar relationship was observed between CD44 and CD69 and plasma GFAP (Figure 5C). Of note, the levels of EV markers did not significantly differ between the whole groups (eFigure 4). In addition, no significant differences were observed between patients with RRMS treated with NTZ (n = 33) and those receiving dimethylfumarate/glatiramer acetate (n = 12) or left untreated (NT, n = 7) (eFigure 5), suggesting that EV molecular composition changes are not attributable to treatment. In conclusion, our multiplex data suggest that the increase in a subset of EV markers may specifically be associated with low disease activity in RRMS.
