IntroductionCoronary heart disease (CHD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide despite significant advancements in medical treatments and prevention strategies1. CHD, often referred to as coronary artery disease (CAD), arises from atherosclerosis, the build-up of plaque within the coronary arteries, leading to narrowed or blocked arteries and reduced blood flow to the heart muscle2. This condition can manifest as myocardial ischemia, angina, or, in severe cases, myocardial infarction and heart failure. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), CHD is the foremost cause of death globally, responsible for millions of fatalities annually3. This persistent global burden necessitates continued research to identify novel risk factors, improve early detection, and develop effective prevention strategies. Traditional risk factors for CHD, including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, smoking, physical inactivity, obesity, and diabetes mellitus, are well-established and widely studied4. However, the prevalence of CHD remains high, even with advances in managing these factors. This underscores the need to explore additional or emerging risk factors that may contribute to the disease burden. Identifying non-traditional biomarkers that are easily measurable and closely associated with CHD could enhance early diagnosis and risk stratification, thereby improving patient outcomes.In recent years, liver health has emerged as a critical area of interest in cardiovascular disease research. The liver plays a central role in systemic metabolism, including lipid regulation, protein synthesis, detoxification, and energy storage5. Evidence increasingly suggests that liver dysfunction, including fibrosis and fat accumulation, is intricately linked to cardiovascular risk6. Liver stiffness, a key marker of fibrosis, can be non-invasively assessed using transient elastography (TE)7. Elevated liver stiffness, indicative of advanced fibrosis, has been associated with systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and metabolic dysregulation pathophysiological processes that are also integral to CHD development. Liver fibrosis, often a consequence of chronic liver injury from conditions such as viral hepatitis, alcohol use, or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), is a progressive condition linked to cirrhosis and liver failure8. Beyond its hepatic implications, liver fibrosis has been associated with increased cardiovascular mortality. However, the precise mechanisms linking liver stiffness and CHD remain unclear. While some studies support a strong association, others suggest that liver stiffness may act as a surrogate marker for metabolic disorders like obesity and insulin resistance, which independently contribute to CHD9.In parallel, liver fat content, another important marker of liver health, has gained attention in cardiovascular research. MASLD, characterized by hepatic fat accumulation in the absence of significant alcohol consumption, is the most prevalent chronic liver condition globally10. It is strongly associated with metabolic syndrome, obesity, and type 2 diabetes—key risk factors for CHD. Emerging evidence links MASLD to an elevated risk of cardiovascular events, but the specific mechanisms and the dose–response relationship between liver fat and CHD remain underexplored11. Furthermore, the relationship may not be linear, with varying levels of liver fat potentially exerting different impacts on cardiovascular health. Despite the growing recognition of liver health as a contributor to cardiovascular risk, research in this area is complicated by the interrelated nature of metabolic disorders. Liver stiffness and fat accumulation often coexist, making it challenging to disentangle their individual effects on CHD risk. Moreover, their relationship with CHD may exhibit non-linear patterns, where small changes in liver health markers can disproportionately influence cardiovascular risk. Advanced statistical methodologies are required to elucidate these complex, non-linear relationships.Traditional statistical models, such as logistic regression, are frequently employed in epidemiological research but may fail to capture the non-linear associations between predictors and outcomes12. More flexible approaches, such as restricted cubic splines (RCS), can model dose–response relationships and identify thresholds that linear models might overlook13. Additionally, machine learning techniques, including decision trees, support vector machines (SVM), and ensemble methods like XGBoost, offer robust tools for analyzing high-dimensional datasets. These methods can uncover intricate patterns and interactions among variables, enabling more accurate prediction of CHD risk based on liver health markers.Accumulating evidence suggests that liver stiffness and fat accumulation are not only localized hepatic changes but also have systemic implications. Increased liver stiffness, indicative of fibrosis, triggers chronic inflammation via the activation of Kupffer cells and hepatic stellate cells, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and C-reactive protein (CRP)14,15. These inflammatory mediators can impair endothelial function, reduce nitric oxide bioavailability, and promote vascular adhesion, all of which contribute to atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease16. Similarly, excessive hepatic fat accumulation exacerbates insulin resistance and induces oxidative stress, further disrupting glucose and lipid metabolism and promoting endothelial dysfunction and arterial stiffness17. These biological processes provide a mechanistic link between liver pathology and elevated cardiovascular risk.The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) provides a comprehensive, nationally representative dataset for investigating the relationship between liver health and CHD18. NHANES includes detailed clinical, demographic, and imaging data, making it an ideal resource for examining liver stiffness and fat content as potential CHD risk factors. By leveraging NHANES data, researchers can evaluate whether these liver health markers independently predict CHD risk and assess their utility in clinical risk stratification. This study aims to utilize NHANES data from 2017–2023 to explore the relationship between liver stiffness and fat content, as measured by transient elastography, and CHD. Specifically, we seek to determine whether these markers serve as independent risk factors for CHD and assess their predictive value using advanced statistical and machine learning methods. By elucidating the connections between liver health and CHD, this research intends to provide novel biomarkers and tools for early detection and risk stratification, thereby informing targeted prevention and intervention strategies for cardiovascular disease.
