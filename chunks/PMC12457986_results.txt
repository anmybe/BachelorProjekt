RESULTSThe best model without tau PET used pTau217, clinical, and MRI data (R 2 = 0.32; area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) for classifying a 0.5‐point PACC decline = 78.6%). Replacing MRI with tau PET improved performance (R 2 = 0.42; AUROC = 83.1%). Predicted trajectories as a prognostic covariate reduced sample sizes by 35% and increased power from 80% to 94.7%. 3RESULTS3.1Study population and cognitive trajectoriesThe baseline characteristics of the 1023 participants in the A4 Study (519 placebo, 504 solanezumab) with complete amyloid PET, clinical, plasma pTau217, MRI, and longitudinal PACC data are presented in Table 1. Participants had a mean age of 71.9 years (SD 4.8), with 58.5% female and 94.4% White. The average education level was 16.6 years (SD 2.7). Overall, 59.3% carried at least one APOE ε4 allele, with 51.0% heterozygous and 8.3% homozygous. Baseline amyloid burden, measured by amyloid PET CL, averaged 66.0 (SD 32.6), plasma pTau217 was 0.28 (SD 0.16), PACC was 0.05 (SD 2.7), and MMSE was 28.8 (SD 1.3). No significant differences were observed between placebo and solanezumab groups (p > 0.05), ensuring balanced cohorts for analysis.Longitudinal PACC trajectories (Figure 2A) revealed substantial heterogeneity in individual cognitive changes over the 240‐week study period, with some participants maintaining stable cognition and others showing varying rates of decline, particularly after week 192. This variability highlights the complexity of predicting continuous cognitive trajectories in preclinical AD. In scenarios without tau PET data, Model‐1, which included clinical assessments (PACC and its four components, CDR‐SB, ADCS‐ADL, and CFI), plasma pTau217, and MRI‐derived structural brain network (SBN) modules and regional hubs, demonstrated robust performance for predicting continuous PACC change. Adding MRI‐derived features to Model‐2 did not improve accuracy meaningfully (R 2 = 0.430, 0.458, and 0.413 across timepoints), indicating that once tau PET is available, structural MRI adds limited incremental value. As single‐split evaluations can be sensitive to the specific data partition [refs. 56, 57, 58], the repeated cross‐validation framework ensures robust performance estimates.3.3Enhancing trial efficiency with prognostic covariatesTo evaluate the impact of prognostic covariates on trial efficiency, we performed 5000 simulations using APCs designed to span a broad range of predictive strengths, with R 2 values up to 0.42. These simulations replicated the data structure and dropout patterns observed in the A4 study, and assessed how incorporating APCs with varying predictive strengths influenced treatment effect estimation within the MMRM analyses.As shown in Figure 5A and Table 3, increasing levels of predictive strength (R 2) of the APCs led to substantial gains in statistical power and reductions in variance of the treatment effect estimate. With a fixed sample size of 504 participants per group, a nonpredictive APC (R 2 = 0) yielded 80.2% power and, as expected, no reduction in variance. In contrast, incorporating Model‐1's APC reduced variance by 27%, increasing power to 90.6%, while Model‐2's APC achieved a 38.1% variance reduction, further improving power to 94.7%. These improvements scaled continuously with R2, demonstrating the value of incorporating even moderately predictive covariates. Figure 5B illustrates the corresponding impact on sample size requirements when power is fixed at 80% and type I error at 5%. Model‐1's APC reduced the required sample size per group by 23.8% (from 504 to 384 participants), and Model‐2's APC achieved a 34.9% reduction (to 328 participants). While less efficient than models incorporating MRI or tau PET, these results demonstrate that Model‐5 can nonetheless yield meaningful trial efficiency gains.Together, these findings demonstrate that incorporating APCs into trial analyses can substantially enhance statistical efficiency. Model‐1 offers practical benefits in settings where tau PET is unavailable, whereas Model‐2 yields greater gains when tau PET data are available, enabling more precise and powerful detection of treatment effects with fewer participants. Similarly, Model‐3 and Model‐4 are suitable options when pTau217 data are not available, and Model‐5 is a suitable option when imaging data (MRI and PET) are not available.These reductions in sample size requirements also yield substantial benefits for participant screening, a major hurdle in preclinical AD trials. In the A4 study, 82.7% of screened individuals did not meet the inclusion criteria, implying that approximately six individuals had to be screened to enroll one participant. 36 Thus, the 23.8% reduction in sample size achieved with Model‐1's APC corresponds to approximately 1,387 fewer individuals needing to be screened. Similarly, the 34.9% reduction enabled by Model‐2's APC translates to 2034 fewer individuals requiring screening. These findings highlight the practical advantages of incorporating prognostic covariates, not only in improving statistical power and precision but also in significantly easing recruitment demands in preclinical AD trials.
