Background Limited ancestral diversity has impaired our ability to detect risk variants more prevalent in ancestry groups of predominantly non-European ancestral background in genome-wide association studies (GWAS). We construct and analyze a multi-ancestry GWAS dataset in the Alzheimer’s Disease Genetics Consortium (ADGC) to test for novel shared and population-specific late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (LOAD) susceptibility loci and evaluate underlying genetic architecture in 37,382 non-Hispanic White (NHW), 6728 African American, 8899 Hispanic (HIS), and 3232 East Asian individuals, performing within ancestry fixed-effects meta-analysis followed by a cross-ancestry random-effects meta-analysis. BackgroundAlzheimer’s disease (AD) affects over a third of people aged 80 years or older , with AD prevalence continuing to increase with the growth in the global population of elderly. Currently, there are approximately 50 million persons with AD worldwide, projected to increase to 135–200 million by 2050 . Coupled with the facts that there are limited therapeutic interventions for AD and that this disease affects individuals of all ancestries, the expanding burden of AD makes this disease an urgent global public health crisis. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified over 75 risk loci associated with risk of AD and related dementias [2–5], providing critical insights into molecular mechanisms underlying disease development. Most of these studies, however, have been conducted in populations of predominantly European ancestral background, although it is clear similar genetic ancestral background impacts genotypic risk , with rare variants in particular usually being more frequently shared between populations with greater genetic similarity. This has limited the ability to identify variants and loci specific to non-European ancestral backgrounds [7–9], determine shared genetic risk and protective factors across diverse populations , and to capitalize on differential linkage disequilibrium (LD) structures across varied ancestral genetic backgrounds to improve fine-mapping of causal loci [11–14]. Thus, progress towards genetic prediction and precision medicine is significantly hampered by lack of data from populations with significant non-European background . To characterize genetic ancestral background  while retaining statistical power to detect associations, our study examined genetic clustering of samples (excluding within-study population outliers) at the dataset level against 1000 Genomes/Human Genome Diversity Project reference panels to identify unique GSGs for population-specific analyses. As noted in the National Academies for the Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine report on the use of population descriptors in genetic studies  and related guidances , the assignment of samples to GSGs may be constrained by the limitations of the datasets used, particularly “legacy” datasets as most of our studies were. Assignment to GSGs at the dataset level allowed us to account for multiple sources of between-study heterogeneity, like study design, genotyping platform, ascertainment approach, and covariate collection in our > 60 legacy datasets. GSG classifications used multiple criteria. Studies predominantly clustering in principal components analysis (PCA) only with populations of European ancestral background sets were labeled non-Hispanic whites (NHW)); studies predominantly clustering with only with populations of East Asian ancestral background were labeled East Asian (EAS); studies whose samples clustered between and across European, Amerindian, and African ancestral backgrounds with ascertainment in heavily Spanish-speaking countries, regions, or areas and incorporating individuals identified as Latino were labeled Hispanic (HIS); and finally, studies whose samples clustered between and across European and African ancestral backgrounds with ascertainment in the USA and incorporating individuals identified as non-Hispanic black were labeled African American (AFA). This approach facilitated sufficiently well-powered discovery of novel loci across a spectrum of diverse ancestral genetic backgrounds.To identify novel loci and explore differences in genetic association across these groups, the Alzheimer’s Disease Genetics Consortium (ADGC) initiated the first large-scale multi-ancestry GWAS meta-analysis with direct genotyping and either clinical evaluation of or pathological confirmation of AD status on over 56,000 individuals across four genetic similarity groups (GSGs) with diverse ancestral genetic backgrounds, including 6728 AFA, 8899 HIS, 3232 EAS, and 37,382 NHW participants. We analyzed imputed genotypes derived from the diverse Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed) R5 haplotype reference panel containing over 300 million unique variants from a large, diverse sample set. We conducted a two-tier analysis including a cross-GSG GWAS meta-analysis of AD and secondary analyses examining novel within-GSG loci including cross-GSG comparisons of GSG-specific associations to understand genetic architecture underlying novel genetic loci and to identify shared and distinct differences of AD risk and protective loci in populations of diverse ancestral genetic background.
