IntroductionMalaria is a vector-borne disease caused by parasites of the Plasmodium genus. Of these, Plasmodium falciparum is the most common species and responsible for the largest public health burden worldwide.1 P. falciparum parasites are transmitted from one person to another by female Anopheles mosquitoes. Transition of the malaria parasites from the mosquito to the human host and from humans back into mosquitoes constitute two bottlenecks in the malaria parasite’s lifecycle. These bottlenecks are attractive targets for vaccination due to the low number of parasites, their extracellular location, and limited diversity of antigens accessible to the immune system.The development of transmission-blocking vaccines (TBVs) against P. falciparum malaria is recognized as a key goal to achieving and sustaining malaria elimination,2 as most malaria cases are asymptomatic and therefore relatively unaffected by current malaria control interventions that aim to reduce the disease burden.Malaria TBVs aim to elicit antibodies that are taken up by feeding mosquitos together with sexual stage parasites. Within the mosquito midgut, vaccine elicited antibodies bind to the native parasite proteins thereby inhibiting parasite multiplication and onwards transmission.3,4 The sexual-stage antigen Pfs48/45 is an attractive candidate for a TBV since it is involved in male fertility5 and since it is a target of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs)6 and polyclonal antibodies7,8 with the capacity to prevent transmissio n of parasites to mosquitoes in the standard membrane feeding assay (SMFA), which assesses transmission-reducing activity (TRA) ex vivo.6,9,10 Additionally, immune epidemiological studies conducted in Africa7,11–15 and other regions16,17 have demonstrated that Pfs48/45 is recognized by naturally acquired antibodies and that high levels of anti-Pfs48/45 IgG antibodies were associated with an ability to reduce malaria transmission to mosquitoes in the SMFA.7,15 The clinical relevance of TBVs based on Pfs48/45 has been confirmed in Phase 1 studies where they elicited high levels of TRA.18,19While TBVs may reduce malaria transmission, such vaccines do not offer the vaccinated individual any direct protection, as they do not alleviate clinical manifestations of malaria. It is therefore desirable to combine a TBV with an anti-infection vaccine-target such as the Circumsporozoite protein (CSP). Hence, ProC6C also contains immune dominant sequences of CSP in addition to those derived from Pfs48/45 and Pfs230.20 A Phase 1 clinical trial performed in Burkinabe adults evaluated ProC6C in two adjuvant combinations, Aluminum hydroxide alone (ProC6C-AlOH) and aluminum hydroxide together with the saponin based Matrix-M™ adjuvant (ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M). In this trial, we found that ProC6C vaccines were safe and immunogenic and that ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M elicited the highest levels of vaccine-specific IgG.18 Previous analysis at two-weeks post third dose (D70), showed that ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M elicited high levels of transmission-reducing antibodies18 as well as high levels of CSP-specific antibodies that were also found to be functional in preventing liver invasion.21 Those CSP-specific antibodies were also evaluated for longevity, and while the response decayed, the anti-CSP IgG concentration remained similar to those vaccinated with RTS,S/AS01 through D180.21Exploratory endpoints analyzed during the Phase 1 clinical trial of ProC6C indicated a decrease in vaccine-specific antibody titer beyond D180 (four months post last dose of the 0, 1, 2-month regimen).18 However as longevity of transmission blocking (TB) antibodies is key to vaccine development, we further investigated the specificity, kinetics, and duration of transmission-reducing antibodies (Pfs48/45 and Pfs230) elicited by ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M from the Phase 1 trial in Burkinabe adults.
