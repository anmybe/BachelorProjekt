METHODSModels were trained on longitudinal Preclinical Alzheimer's Cognitive Composite (PACC) data up to 240 weeks from the Phase III A4 study of solanezumab. Baseline inputs included demographics, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4, clinical scores, amyloid positron emission tomography (PET), plasma pTau217, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and tau PET (sub‐study). Stochastic gradient boosting was used, with evaluation via cross‐validation and trial simulations. 2METHODS2.1DatabaseData were obtained from the A4 Study (NCT02008357), a multicenter, placebo‐controlled Phase III clinical trial to evaluate the efficacy of solanezumab in preventing cognitive decline among cognitively normal, amyloid‐positive older adults. The trial, which enrolled 1169 individuals aged 65–86 years with elevated brain amyloid levels, did not demonstrate a statistically significant treatment benefit. 36 For the present analysis, progression models were trained using data from both the placebo (n = 519) and treatment arms (n = 504), after excluding individuals with missing baseline variables. This resulted in a final sample of 1023 participants for models that did not include tau PET data. For models incorporating tau PET, a subset of 339 participants (placebo: n = 170; treatment: n = 169) with complete tau imaging data was used. Model training was conducted on the full dataset, with treatment arm assignment included as a predictor.The A4 Study was approved by the Institutional Review Board or independent ethics committees at each site. All participants and their study partners provided written informed consent before data collection, which included consent for data sharing. The PACC is a four‐component composite measure designed to capture amyloid‐related cognitive decline in preclinical AD. 37 It combines performance on the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), 38 Logical Delayed Episodic Learning (LDEL), 39 , 40 Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST), 41 and Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test (FCSRT). 42 Three versions of the component subtests were alternated to minimize practice effects, and testing was conducted by trained site psychometrists blinded to treatment arm assignment and adverse events. Each component score was converted to a z‐score by subtracting the baseline mean and dividing by the baseline standard deviation for that component. 37 The PACC score was calculated as the average of the four z‐scores, with negative scores indicating cognitive decline.The baseline clinical features utilized as predictors in the progression models included the PACC and its four components, Clinical Dementia Rating Scale–Sum of Boxes (CDR–SB), 43 and both participant and partner versions of the Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study Activities of Daily Living (ADCS‐ADL) 44 and the Cognitive Function Index (CFI). 45 All eligible participants underwent structural T1‐weighted MRI and 18F‐Florbetapir amyloid PET. The MRI scans were processed using FreeSurfer Version 7.3.1 to extract brain MRI measures, including cortical volume, surface area, and thickness, across multiple regions of interest according to the Desikan–Killiany atlas. 46 This process resulted in a total of 207 regional brain measures, with cortical thickness reported in millimeters (mm), volume in cubic millimeters (mm3), and area in square millimeters (mm2). All measures were normalized by intracranial volume to adjust for differences in head size. To enhance analysis efficiency and focus on relevant data, the baseline structural MRI features utilized by the prediction models were limited to the 18 structural brain network (SBN) modules and 36 hub regional measures identified in the recent publication. 29 This approach effectively reduced the redundancy and the number of prediction features.Amyloid‐PET uptake was measured using mean cortical SUVR with a cerebellar reference, as previously described. 47 Amyloid status for A4 eligibility was assessed using an algorithm that combined quantitative SUVR methods with qualitative visual read performed at a central laboratory. SUVR values were converted to the standard Centiloid (CL) scale 48 and included as a baseline predictor in the prediction models.Tau PET imaging with 18F‐flortaucipir (FTP) was available for a subset of 339 participants included in this analysis. The data were processed using the Stanford Tau PET Pipeline. 49 T1‐weighted MRI scans were processed with FreeSurfer to generate cortical segmentations. PET frames were realigned and summed, with additional smoothing (4 mm full width at half maximum [FWHM]) applied to noisy scans from a subset of participants. PET images were co‐registered to MRI using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM) software and regional mean uptake values were extracted from the FreeSurfer‐defined regions. Bilateral regions were computed via volume‐weighted averaging across hemispheres. All tau PET values were normalized to a gray matter‐only cerebellar cortex reference region. In the progression models, all bilateral regional SUVRs were included as predictors, derived from cortical and subcortical gray matter regions defined by the FreeSurfer Desikan–Killiany atlas. Regions were excluded if they exhibited off‐target binding (e.g., choroid plexus, brainstem), nonspecific signal (e.g., white matter), or were structurally irrelevant or used as the reference region (e.g., ventricles, cerebellum).pTau217 concentrations were quantified on plasma samples using a validated electrochemiluminescence (ECL) immunoassay, performed on the Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) Sector S 600 MM platform at the CAP‐accredited, CLIA‐certified Lilly Clinical Diagnostics Laboratory. 50 2.3Data analysis2.3.1Prediction model development and evaluationPrognostic models were developed to predict individual‐level trajectories of PACC change over 4.5 years (240 weeks) using the Stochastic Gradient Boosting Machine (SGBM) algorithm. 51 Models incorporated various combinations of baseline predictors, including clinical assessments, global amyloid PET CL, plasma pTau217, MRI‐derived SBN modules and regional hubs, and bilateral SUVRs from cortical and subcortical gray matter regions on tau PET (excluding regions with potential off‐target binding or nonspecific signal). Tau PET regions exhibiting off‐target binding or nonspecific signal, such as the choroid plexus, brainstem, ventricles, white matter, and cerebellum (used as reference) were excluded. All models additionally included demographic variables (age, sex, and education), apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allelic count, treatment arm, and PACC test version, with actual visit time (often delayed due to coronavirus disease 2019 [COVID‐19]) modeled as a continuous variable.SGBM was selected for this study due to its demonstrated effectiveness in modeling cognitive decline in early AD 29 and clinical trial populations, 33 as well as its capacity to handle high‐dimensional data such as MRI, tau PET, and clinical assessments. SGBM is an ensemble learning method that aggregates predictions from multiple classification and regression trees (CART) to improve prediction accuracy. It can capture complex nonlinear effects and interactions among predictors without requiring explicit distributional assumptions or prespecified functional forms. Overfitting is controlled through internal tuning using cross‐validation. In this study, models were configured with up to 500 trees and allowed interactions up to the fifth order in base CART. Predictor importance was quantified based on the reduction in mean squared error (MSE) attributed to each variable, normalized to a 0%–100% scale. 52 Relationships between predictors and outcomes, including nonlinear effects and interactions, were explored using individual conditional expectation (ICE) profiles and partial dependence plots (PDP). 53 Unlike parametric regression models, which require prespecified model structures, the SGBM automatically identified influential predictors (e.g., pTau217, specific regional tau PET SUVRs) and enabled interpretable visualizations through ICE and PDP interaction plots.Models were trained using both placebo and treatment arm participants, with treatment arm assignment included as a covariate. To evaluate prediction of natural cognitive decline, performance was assessed exclusively in held‐out placebo participants from the test folds. As illustrated in Figure 1, we employed a repeated five‐fold cross‐validation framework: in each iteration, the dataset was randomly partitioned into five folds, with each fold held out once for testing and the remaining four used for training. This process was repeated 50 times to account for variability due to data partitioning and to ensure robust performance estimates. Within each iteration, predictions for placebo participants across the five test folds were aggregated and compared to observed PACC outcomes. Continuous predictions of PACC change were evaluated using robust Huber‐type R2, 54 Harrell's concordance index (C‐index), 55 and median absolute error (MAE). For binary classification of cognitive decline, thresholds of −0.25 and −0.5 in PACC change were applied, corresponding to declines of at least 0.25 points and 0.5 points, respectively. Classification performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). All reported performance metrics were calculated separately for each of the 50 cross‐validation iterations and summarized by their median values, providing stable and generalizable estimates of model performance. A4, Anti‐Amyloid Treatment in Asymptomatic Alzheimer's Study; PACC, Preclinical Alzheimer's Cognitive Composite.2.3.2Application of baseline‐predicted PACC decline as APCA central application explored in this study is the use of baseline‐predicted individual cognitive trajectories, derived from prognostic models, as a time‐varying APC in the MMRM analysis. This approach aims to improve the precision of treatment effect estimates in clinical trials and, consequently, enhance statistical power and reduce required sample sizes when applied prospectively in trial design.To evaluate this, we conducted a simulation study examining how time‐varying APCs with varying strengths of association with clinical outcomes influence MMRM performance. A total of 5000 synthetic clinical trial datasets were generated using a mixture of multivariate normal distributions, calibrated to approximately match the means, variances, correlation structures, and distribution skewness observed in the A4 Study. Simulated trials replicated real‐world missing data patterns, including dropout rates of 3%, 7%, 14%, 20%, and 30% at weeks 48, 96, 144, 192, and 240, respectively. Participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to placebo or active treatment arms, with each trial spanning 240 weeks.Placebo group trajectories were simulated to align with observed PACC changes among participants with amyloid PET CL greater than 40 in the A4 Study. This threshold was applied in the simulation to define a preclinical AD population with sufficiently elevated amyloid levels. The treatment group was simulated to exhibit a slower rate of decline, reaching 62% of the placebo group's rate of decline by week 240. Additionally, APOE ε4 status and baseline PACC score were simulated to reflect their distributions in the A4 cohort and were included as covariates in the MMRM, along with treatment group and interaction terms for APC by time, and treatment by time. The APC used in the MMRM was constructed by fixing the treatment indicator at placebo in the prediction model trained via the SBGM algorithm. Time was modeled as a fixed categorical effect rather than a continuous variable.Nine simulation scenarios were tested, each varying the predictive strength of the APC at week 240, quantified by a distinct R 2 value ranging from 0 to 0.42. The temporal pattern of APC–outcome association was modeled to increase over time, consistent with trends observed in the A4 data. Two key outcomes were assessed: (1) the sample size required to detect a 38% slowing in cognitive decline at week 240 with 80% power and a two‐sided 5% Type I error rate, and (2) the statistical power to detect the same effect with a fixed sample size of 504 participants per arm. We assumed a 38% slowing of PACC decline for trial simulations, consistent with prevention–trial literature. 59 A 40%–50% slowing on PACC has been shown to delay progression from cognitively unimpaired to early mild cognitive impairment (MCI) by ∼3 years, a clinically meaningful difference. Our assumption of a 38% decline is closely aligned with this established range. This simulation framework enabled a rigorous evaluation of how incorporating APCs with varying predictive accuracy can enhance trial efficiency while maintaining statistical validity.All analyses were performed using R Version 4.5 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), 60 with specific packages gbm3, 61 pdp, 62 mmrm, 63 and emeans. 64 2.3Data analysis2.3.1Prediction model development and evaluationPrognostic models were developed to predict individual‐level trajectories of PACC change over 4.5 years (240 weeks) using the Stochastic Gradient Boosting Machine (SGBM) algorithm. 51 Models incorporated various combinations of baseline predictors, including clinical assessments, global amyloid PET CL, plasma pTau217, MRI‐derived SBN modules and regional hubs, and bilateral SUVRs from cortical and subcortical gray matter regions on tau PET (excluding regions with potential off‐target binding or nonspecific signal).
