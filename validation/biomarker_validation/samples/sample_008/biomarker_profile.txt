Activity Context
----------------
The included studies collectively cover a wide range of athletic scenarios: aerobically trained individuals completing a half-marathon, average-trained men performing exhaustive treadmill exercise, elite athletes in high-intensity functional Cross Combat, elite Olympic beach volleyball athletes during simulated competition, ultra-elite breath-hold divers engaging in maximal static apnea, professional male soccer players in pre-season training, elite male rowers undertaking strenuous ergometer sessions, and trained CrossFit® athletes following a 6-week program. Additionally, two studies address non-athletic populations: patients undergoing allogeneic stem-cell transplantation and adult dengue patients.

Effect Summary
--------------
Hematocrit (Hct) exhibits various responses to exercise: it tends to increase after exhaustive treadmill exercise, intense beach volleyball matches, and strenuous rowing, primarily reflecting acute hemoconcentration due to fluid shifts. Conversely, a transient decrease was noted during recovery from high-intensity functional Cross Combat, attributed to hydration restoration, while remaining largely stable during the exercise itself. During strenuous rowing, Hct consistently increased during exercise and decreased during recovery. In ultra-elite breath-hold divers, Hct was elevated under hyperoxic apnea conditions, likely due to splenic contraction or plasma volume contraction. No significant group differences were observed with Montmorency tart cherry supplementation during a half-marathon, and Hct remained unchanged after 6 weeks of Tribulus terrestris supplementation combined with CrossFit training. One study on professional soccer players noted Hct as a parameter but did not report its changes. In non-athletic contexts, Hct decreased at engraftment post-allogeneic stem-cell transplantation and showed no significant change in dengue patients across different severity groups.

Sport Implications
------------------
Hematocrit serves as a useful biomarker for monitoring hydration status, acute blood volume changes, and physiological stress in athletes. An increase during or immediately post-exercise often indicates hemoconcentration, which can stem from dehydration or fluid shifts, influencing blood viscosity and oxygen transport efficiency. Its decline during recovery suggests effective rehydration and plasma volume restoration. For disciplines like breath-hold diving, an elevated Hct (e.g., due to splenic contraction) signifies an adaptive mechanism to enhance oxygen-carrying capacity, directly impacting performance. Consistently stable Hct levels across a training block or with specific supplementation can indicate adequate adaptation and fluid balance, implying the training load is well-managed without causing chronic hemoconcentration or hemodilution. Hct is also critical for adjusting other biomarker concentrations to account for fluid shifts. The findings from studies on allogeneic stem-cell transplantation and dengue infection have no direct implications for training, recovery, or performance in healthy athletes.
