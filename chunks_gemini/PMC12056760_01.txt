Background and Objectives Multiple sclerosis (MS), neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody–associated disease (MOGAD) are autoimmune disorders of the CNS causing severe neurologic impairment. Evidence suggests that extracellular vesicles (EVs) may play a disease-specific role in the orchestration of the immune cell response of MS, NMOSD, and MOGAD. In addition, EVs are considered as a potential source of biomarkers that may allow us to establish molecular signatures for these diseases and perhaps as well to follow treatment effects and disease progression. The aim of this study was to analyze the composition of EVs in patients with relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS) (n = 52), NMOSD (n = 19), and MOGAD (n = 10) and healthy controls ([HCs], n = 15). Methods The concentrations of neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) were determined in plasma using single-molecule array (SIMOA). The size and concentration of tetraspanin-presenting EVs were evaluated in plasma samples with a single-particle interferometric resonance imaging sensor (SP-IRIS). Tetraspanin-independent analyses were performed by nanoparticle-tracking analysis (NTA) after EV isolation by size exclusion (SmartSEC) and cryo-electron microscopy observations. EV epitopes were analyzed by extended multiplex analysis using flow cytometry. Results The plasma concentration of NfL and GFAP was significantly higher in patients with RRMS than in HCs. For patients with NMOSD, only GFAP increased. The density of EVs assessed by NTA was lower in plasma of patients with RRMS than in HC plasma. In addition, the 3 disease groups presented increased mean EV sizes in comparison with HCs. Tetraspanin-based EV analyses by SP-IRIS allowed us to observe a modest difference in the level of CD81 in RRMS EVs. In patients with RRMS, but not in those with NMOSD and MOGAD, multiplex/flow cytometry analyses revealed changes in the EV levels of CD29, CD31, and CD69 associated with the time elapsed since the last relapse. The negative correlations established between the vesicular levels of CD31, CD40, CD44, CD49c, CD69, and NfL or GFAP z-scores suggest a negative relationship specifically in RRMS. Discussion We speculate that the higher release of EVs containing CD29, CD31, CD40, CD44, CD49c, and CD69 in plasma, at low levels of circulating NfL/GFAP, may be associated with reduced immune cell activity in RRMS.       "text": "Introduction Multiple sclerosis (MS), neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody–associated disease (MOGAD) are chronic autoimmune demyelinating diseases of the CNS. They present overlapping motor, sensory, and cognitive symptoms, such as vision and walking impairment, respectively, caused by optic nerve and spinal cord lesions. 1 In contrast to MS, where no specific autoantibody profile has been established, current diagnosis of NMOSD and MOGAD heavily relies on serum autoantibodies. 2,3 Although AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG greatly facilitate diagnosis, their blood concentrations do not correlate with disease activity or treatment effects. By contrast, the serum levels of neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), reflecting neuronal damage and MS progression, respectively, have been linked to disease activity. 4,5 However, these molecules are not disease-specific. 6,7 Therefore, more sensitive and specific tools are required for improving diagnosis and follow-up of disease course. In recent years, cell-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as a promising source of disease biomarkers in blood and CSF. 8,9 EVs are small bilayered membranous particles ranging between approximately 50 and 1,000 nm in diameter. By delivering proteins, lipids, and mRNA, EVs contribute to the intercellular communication. 10 In the immune system, cytokines, chemokines, coagulation factors, and antigens enclosed in T-cell–shed EVs are important mediators between the innate and adaptive immune response. 11,12 Notably, high levels of T-cell–derived EVs in serum and CSF are associated with MS relapse. 13,14 Whether immune cells release EVs in different amounts and of different compositions in MS, NMOSD, and MOGAD is not known. Moreover, CNS cells also secrete EVs in MS; myelin-specific proteins, such as myelin basic protein (MBP) and MOG, have been detected in EVs isolated from the plasma and CSF of patients with MS, suggesting that oligodendrocyte-shed EVs cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and reach the circulation. 15 In addition, the level of MBP-positive and MOG-positive EVs correlated with disability measured using Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) scores. 16,17 Very little is known about the protein cargoes of EVs in NMOSD and MOGAD. In NMOSD relapse, astrocytes release specific microRNAs (miRNAs) encapsulated in EVs that are detectable in the blood. 18,19 Importantly, the authors showed that some of these miRNAs induced demyelinating lesions in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo. 19 At the protein level, it has been suggested that GFAP and complement factor C4b-binding protein α-chain may be enriched in CSF-derived EVs in patients with NMOSD compared with MS, but further investigations are necessary to confirm these observations. 20 Comparative analyses in MS vs NMOSD vs MOGAD are missing to determine whether EV proteins differ in a disease-specific fashion. The aim of this study was to characterize circulating EVs in patients with MS, NMOSD, and MOGAD in parallel. It is important to underline that EVs are heterogeneous particles, of variable sizes and cellular origins, resulting from different biogenesis mechanisms (cell membrane budding and fusion of multivesicular endosomes). Therefore, throughout this study, the nonrestrictive term “EVs” encompassing all categories of vesicles was preferred, as recommended by the International Society of Extracellular Vesicles and the MISEV2023 Guidelines. 21 In the membrane of EVs, CD9, CD63, and CD81 tetraspanins are considered as specific markers. 22 Nevertheless, the level of tetraspanins varies between cell types, tissues, and diseases. 23 For this reason, tetraspanin-dependent and independent methods were used to examine EVs. An extended analysis of 37 EV epitopes revealed specific enrichments in MS samples by flow cytometry. Methods Ethics, Participants, and Plasma Collection A total of 96 participants were included in this study. Patients diagnosed with relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS) (n = 52), AQP4-positive NMOSD (n = 19), and MOG-positive MOGAD (n = 10) and age-matched and sex-matched healthy controls ([HCs], n = 15) from the Eginition University Hospital were included in this study. The study was approved by the ethical committee of the Eginition University Hospital (#501/30.7.2019). All patients signed an informed consent form for blood sample use for research purposes. Blood was collected into EDTA tubes and centrifuged within 1–5 hours after blood draw, at 3,100 × g, for 7 minutes at room temperature (RT). Plasma was then aliquoted and stored at −80°C until use. Overview of the Workflow for Plasma Analyses Circulating NfL and GFAP were assayed in plasma samples using single-molecule array (SIMOA) ( Figure 1A ). We then used orthogonal methods to analyze plasma EVs, in accordance with current recommendations (MISEV2023), and evaluated EV characteristics with different methods. We assessed EV size, density, and their subcategories based on tetraspanin expression with a single-particle interferometric resonance imaging sensor (SP-IRIS) using the novel ExoView R100 system (Unchained Labs, Pleasanton, CA) ( Figure 1B ). EVs isolated with a size-exclusion chromatography-based technique were characterized by cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), Western blotting, and nanoparticle-tracking analysis (NTA) ( Figure 1C ). The multiplex antibody bead–based flow cytometry assay allowed simultaneous analysis of 37 cell-specific EV surface epitopes using the MACSPlex EV kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) (Figure 1C). Ultrasensitive Detection of NfL and GFAP With SIMOA NfL and GFAP were measured in plasma in duplicates with a SIMOA, using the Neurology 2-plex B assay (Quanterix, Billerica, MA).
