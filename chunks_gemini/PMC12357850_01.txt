This study aimed to investigate the relationship between liver stiffness measurements (LSM), controlled attenuation parameter (CAP), and coronary heart disease (CHD) using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). A total of 12,684 American populations who underwent health examinations were included from the NHANES database spanning 2017–2020 (pre-pandemic) and 2021–2023. Logistic regression was employed to analyze the associations between LSM, CAP, and CHD. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was used to evaluate the dose–response relationship between LSM, CAP, and CHD. Stratified analyses were performed according to age, sex, race, education level, income, BMI, blood pressure, hepatitis B surface antibody, and cholesterol to explore potential interactions and identify specific subpopulations at risk. Finally, predictive models were developed using logistic regression, decision trees, XGBoost classifiers, and neural networks to assess the predictive value of LSM and CAP for CHD risk. During the study period, 12,684 participants were included, of whom 539 (4.24%) were diagnosed with CHD. After adjusting for confounders, both LSM and CAP were found to be significantly positively associated with CHD risk in U.S. adults. Subgroup analyses revealed a significant positive association between LSM and CHD incidence in participants stratified by race and blood parameters (interaction P -value < 0.050). Additionally, sex, age, and lipid profiles showed a significant positive correlation between CAP and CHD risk. Predictive models also indicated a positive relationship between LSM, CAP, and CHD risk. Both LSM and CAP were nonlinearly and positively associated with CHD risk, highlighting the importance of maintaining lower levels of liver stiffness and fat as a potential preventive strategy for CHD. CHD, often referred to as coronary artery disease (CAD), arises from atherosclerosis, the build-up of plaque within the coronary arteries, leading to narrowed or blocked arteries and reduced blood flow to the heart muscle 2 . This condition can manifest as myocardial ischemia, angina, or, in severe cases, myocardial infarction and heart failure. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), CHD is the foremost cause of death globally, responsible for millions of fatalities annually 3 . This persistent global burden necessitates continued research to identify novel risk factors, improve early detection, and develop effective prevention strategies. Traditional risk factors for CHD, including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, smoking, physical inactivity, obesity, and diabetes mellitus, are well-established and widely studied 4 . However, the prevalence of CHD remains high, even with advances in managing these factors.       "text": "In recent years, liver health has emerged as a critical area of interest in cardiovascular disease research. The liver plays a central role in systemic metabolism, including lipid regulation, protein synthesis, detoxification, and energy storage 5 . Evidence increasingly suggests that liver dysfunction, including fibrosis and fat accumulation, is intricately linked to cardiovascular risk 6 . Liver stiffness, a key marker of fibrosis, can be non-invasively assessed using transient elastography (TE) 7 . Elevated liver stiffness, indicative of advanced fibrosis, has been associated with systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and metabolic dysregulation pathophysiological processes that are also integral to CHD development. Liver fibrosis, often a consequence of chronic liver injury from conditions such as viral hepatitis, alcohol use, or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), is a progressive condition linked to cirrhosis and liver failure 8 . Beyond its hepatic implications, liver fibrosis has been associated with increased cardiovascular mortality. However, the precise mechanisms linking liver stiffness and CHD remain unclear. While some studies support a strong association, others suggest that liver stiffness may act as a surrogate marker for metabolic disorders like obesity and insulin resistance, which independently contribute to CHD 9 . In parallel, liver fat content, another important marker of liver health, has gained attention in cardiovascular research. MASLD, characterized by hepatic fat accumulation in the absence of significant alcohol consumption, is the most prevalent chronic liver condition globally 10 . It is strongly associated with metabolic syndrome, obesity, and type 2 diabetes—key risk factors for CHD. Emerging evidence links MASLD to an elevated risk of cardiovascular events, but the specific mechanisms and the dose–response relationship between liver fat and CHD remain underexplored 11 . Furthermore, the relationship may not be linear, with varying levels of liver fat potentially exerting different impacts on cardiovascular health. Despite the growing recognition of liver health as a contributor to cardiovascular risk, research in this area is complicated by the interrelated nature of metabolic disorders. Liver stiffness and fat accumulation often coexist, making it challenging to disentangle their individual effects on CHD risk. Moreover, their relationship with CHD may exhibit non-linear patterns, where small changes in liver health markers can disproportionately influence cardiovascular risk. Advanced statistical methodologies are required to elucidate these complex, non-linear relationships. Traditional statistical models, such as logistic regression, are frequently employed in epidemiological research but may fail to capture the non-linear associations between predictors and outcomes 12 . More flexible approaches, such as restricted cubic splines (RCS), can model dose–response relationships and identify thresholds that linear models might overlook 13 . Additionally, machine learning techniques, including decision trees, support vector machines (SVM), and ensemble methods like XGBoost, offer robust tools for analyzing high-dimensional datasets. These methods can uncover intricate patterns and interactions among variables, enabling more accurate prediction of CHD risk based on liver health markers. Accumulating evidence suggests that liver stiffness and fat accumulation are not only localized hepatic changes but also have systemic implications. Increased liver stiffness, indicative of fibrosis, triggers chronic inflammation via the activation of Kupffer cells and hepatic stellate cells, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and C-reactive protein (CRP) 14 , 15 . These inflammatory mediators can impair endothelial function, reduce nitric oxide bioavailability, and promote vascular adhesion, all of which contribute to atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease 16 . Similarly, excessive hepatic fat accumulation exacerbates insulin resistance and induces oxidative stress, further disrupting glucose and lipid metabolism and promoting endothelial dysfunction and arterial stiffness 17 . These biological processes provide a mechanistic link between liver pathology and elevated cardiovascular risk. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) provides a comprehensive, nationally representative dataset for investigating the relationship between liver health and CHD 18 . NHANES includes detailed clinical, demographic, and imaging data, making it an ideal resource for examining liver stiffness and fat content as potential CHD risk factors. By leveraging NHANES data, researchers can evaluate whether these liver health markers independently predict CHD risk and assess their utility in clinical risk stratification. This study aims to utilize NHANES data from 2017–2023 to explore the relationship between liver stiffness and fat content, as measured by transient elastography, and CHD. Specifically, we seek to determine whether these markers serve as independent risk factors for CHD and assess their predictive value using advanced statistical and machine learning methods. By elucidating the connections between liver health and CHD, this research intends to provide novel biomarkers and tools for early detection and risk stratification, thereby informing targeted prevention and intervention strategies for cardiovascular disease.
