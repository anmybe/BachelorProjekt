ABSTRACT ProC6C is a multi-stage malaria vaccine designed to disrupt parasite transmission and prevent infection by incorporating three parasite proteins (Pfs230-Pro, Pfs48/45-6C, and CSP) in a single vaccine antigen. The Phase 1 clinical trial (PACTR202201848463189) conducted in Burkina Faso, showed ProC6C-AlOH/Matrix-M was safe, well tolerated, immunogenic and generated a functional antibody response to all three constituent antigens at the primary output (D70). As magnitude and durability are central to an efficacious malaria vaccine, analysis was expanded past the initial endpoint, to determine transmission-blocking antibodies (anti-Pfs230 and anti-Pfs48/45-6C) present through D180. Analysis of transmission-reducing activity (TRA) showed 7/20 samples remained biologically active at D180. To identify immune biomarkers for high levels of TRA, the Pfs48/45-6C IgG concentration (calculated relative to the transmission-blocking mAb TB31F) was compared among TRA positive and negative individuals. The magnitude of anti-Pfs48/45-6C IgG had an excellent predictive accuracy (area under the receiver operating curve [ROC AUC] >0.8) with a threshold of 8.7 μg/ml for significant TRA. Additionally, there was significant correlation of TRA and anti-Pfs48/45 epitope I IgG concentration but not significant correlation for anti-Pfs230-Pro IgG, suggesting that vaccine-induced anti-Pfs48/45-6C IgG is the main predictor of TRA. This finding was corroborated by the observation that complement had no effect on TRA in the standard membrane feeding assay (SMFA). Collectively, these efforts confirm the transmission-blocking attributes of ProC6C and suggest that an alternative dosing regimen be evaluated in future clinical trials to improve longevity of functional transmission-reducing antibodies. The SMFA activity conducted at NIAID was supported by the Division of Intramural Research (DIR), NIAID. Introduction Malaria is a vector-borne disease caused by parasites of the Plasmodium genus. Of these, Plasmodium falciparum is the most common species and responsible for the largest public health burden worldwide. 1 P. falciparum parasites are transmitted from one person to another by female Anopheles mosquitoes. Transition of the malaria parasites from the mosquito to the human host and from humans back into mosquitoes constitute two bottlenecks in the malaria parasite’s lifecycle. These bottlenecks are attractive targets for vaccination due to the low number of parasites, their extracellular location, and limited diversity of antigens accessible to the immune system. The development of transmission-blocking vaccines (TBVs) against P. falciparum malaria is recognized as a key goal to achieving and sustaining malaria elimination, 2 as most malaria cases are asymptomatic and therefore relatively unaffected by current malaria control interventions that aim to reduce the disease burden. Malaria TBVs aim to elicit antibodies that are taken up by feeding mosquitos together with sexual stage parasites. Within the mosquito midgut, vaccine elicited antibodies bind to the native parasite proteins thereby inhibiting parasite multiplication and onwards transmission. 3 , 4 The sexual-stage antigen Pfs48/45 is an attractive candidate for a TBV since it is involved in male fertility 5 and since it is a target of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) 6 and polyclonal antibodies 7 , 8 with the capacity to prevent transmissio n of parasites to mosquitoes in the standard membrane feeding assay (SMFA), which assesses transmission-reducing activity (TRA) ex vivo . 6 , 9 , 10"

Materials and methods Study approval The clinical trial protocol was approved in Burkina Faso by the Ministry of Health Ethical Committee for Biomedical Research (approval reference N°2021-02-034) and the National Regulatory authority, Comité Technique pour les Essais Cliniques, (approval reference N°2022–02041). The study was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and International Conference on Harmonization (ICH) Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines. After study approval by competent authorities, the community assent process was initiated, including local community meetings to explain the trial to potentially eligible adults. Community assent was obtained from the local administrative structures, opinion leaders, family heads, and other community members after explanation and discussion of the study. All study participants provided written approved informed consent before any study procedures were performed and for the analysis conducted. The full inclusion and exclusion criteria are available. 18 Additional details including the full study protocol are available under the initial safety and tolerability publication. 18 Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guidelines 22 were included in the prior publication 18 and also reported in the analysis conducted within this manuscript. The Clinical Trial was registered in the Pan African Clinical Trials Registry (PACTR202201848463189).
