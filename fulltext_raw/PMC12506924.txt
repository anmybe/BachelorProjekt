Background and Objectives
The significance of anti-contactin–associated protein 1 (Caspr1) antibodies in autoimmune nodopathies (ANs) has not been fully established. The aim of this study was to elucidate the clinical profiles of Caspr1 AN.
Methods
Consecutive serum samples were included from patients with chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) nationwide who were referred to our laboratory for antibody testing with questionnaires as part of routine clinical practice and who fulfilled definite European Federation of Neurological Societies and Peripheral Nerve Society electrodiagnostic criteria. Anti-Caspr1 immunoglobulin (Ig) G was screened using in-house ELISA and confirmed using immunohistochemistry and Western blotting. Clinical data, electrophysiologic findings, and treatment responses were retrospectively collected. The pathologic features of sural nerves were also examined. Inflammatory Neuropathy Cause and Treatment (INCAT) disability scores, antibody titers, and serum neurofilament light chain (NfL) levels were analyzed at baseline and follow-up.
Results
Of 559 screened serum samples from patients with CIDP (median age at onset 54 years, 35% female), 19 cases with anti-Caspr1 IgG were identified; the main subclass was IgG4 (17 cases). Patients with IgG4 Caspr1 AN cases exhibited an older age at disease onset (median 67 years); male predominance (82%); high proportions of limb weakness (100%), gait disturbance (100%), tremor (65%), and sensory ataxia (82%); and very high CSF protein levels (249 mg/dL). Nerve conduction studies showed prolonged F-wave and distal motor latencies and reduced sensorimotor conduction velocities in all nerves tested. Light microscopy findings of sural nerve biopsy specimens from 4 patients with Caspr1 AN indicated a loss of myelinated fiber density and myelin ovoids without macrophage-mediated demyelination or onion bulbs. Electron microscopic evaluation demonstrated axo-glial detachment. Patients with IgG4 Caspr1 AN showed a poor response to IVIg (31%) and needed combined immunotherapies. Even after correcting for age, serum NfL levels were higher in patients with IgG4 Caspr1 AN than in healthy controls (
p
= 0.0609) and correlated with INCAT scores (
p
= 0.0143). In the 7 patients with 2 consecutive serum samples, antibody titers decreased with clinical improvement.
Discussion
IgG4 Caspr1 AN presents with a similar clinical phenotype to other nodopathies (e.g., neurofascin 155 and contactin 1), but with an older age at onset. Changes in antibody titers may be a potential biomarker for monitoring disease activity.

Introduction
Autoimmune nodopathies (ANs) are relatively newly proposed immune-mediated neuropathies; they are associated with disease-specific autoantibodies against cell adhesion molecules—such as neurofascin (NF) 186, NF155, contactin 1 (CNTN1), and contactin-associated protein 1 (Caspr1)—located in nodes of Ranvier and paranodes.
1-4
On the basis of their specific clinicopathologic characteristics, ANs were classified separately from chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) in the European Academy of Neurology and Peripheral Nerve Society (EAN/PNS) 2021 guidelines.
4
Recent cohort studies have established the common clinical features of patients with immunoglobulin (Ig) G4 autoantibodies against NF155 and CNTN1; they are characterized by distal-dominant sensorimotor neuropathy, ataxia, highly elevated CSF protein levels, a poor response to IVIg, and a good response to rituximab.
5-12
Moreover, their sural nerve biopsy specimens show axo-glial detachment without macrophage-mediated demyelination, inflammatory cells, or onion bulbs.
13
Patients with anti-Caspr1 IgG (Caspr1 AN) were first described in 2016, and neuropathic pain and cranial nerve involvement were noted as possible specific features.
14,15
However, the clinical profile of Caspr1 AN has not yet been fully elucidated because of its low prevalence and a limited number of case series.
16-25
In this study, we retrospectively screened serum samples from the largest cohort of Japanese patients with CIDP to date, aiming to unveil the clinicolaboratory, electrophysiologic, and pathologic features of Caspr1 AN.
Methods
Participants
Serum samples from patients with immune-mediated neuropathies at multiple medical institutions in Japan were sent to the Department of Neurology at Kyushu University for diagnostic tests of paranodal autoantibodies as part of routine clinical practice, with questionnaires including clinical, laboratory, and electrophysiologic data supplied by treating physicians. Of these, we enrolled consecutive serum samples from patients with CIDP fulfilling the definite European Federation of Neurological Societies (EFNS)/PNS electrodiagnostic criteria between October 2015 and December 2023 (
Figure 1
). In addition, consecutive patients with electrodiagnostically definite CIDP who were followed at Kyushu University Hospital between April 2000 and December 2023 were included. We excluded patients with anti-NF155 IgG (NF155 AN) or without available samples. Screening for anti-CNTN1 IgG was also performed to evaluate double positivity for autoantibodies targeting CNTN1 and Caspr1.
18,26
Patients with anti-CNTN1 IgG were referred to as “CNTN1 AN,” and those with IgG against Caspr1 and CNTN1 were referred to as “Caspr1 and CNTN1 AN.”
26
Autoantibodies against NF155 or CNTN1 were measured using flow cytometric cell-based assay (CBA) with human embryonic kidney 293 cell lines stably expressing human NF155 or CNTN1, as previously described.
6,27
Two additional cases diagnosed as Caspr1 AN in Yamaguchi University
23
(patient 19) and Nagoya University (patient 20) were included for clinical characterization. Serum samples from both patients were referred to the Department of Neurology at Kyushu University to confirm antibody positivity. Serum samples from patients with other peripheral neuropathies, including Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS)/Fisher syndrome (n = 49); anti–myelin-associated glycoprotein neuropathy (n = 11); Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (n = 9); hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsies (n = 1); and polyneuropathy, organomegaly, endocrinopathy, M protein, and skin changes syndrome (n = 9), were used as disease controls. Serum samples of healthy controls (n = 32) were taken from a former study.
6
Clinical and laboratory data were obtained from a questionnaire and medical records. Nerve conduction studies (NCSs) were performed using standard procedures at each institution. We collected the following neurophysiologic data: motor nerve conduction and F-wave studies in the median, ulnar, and tibial nerves and sensory conduction studies in the median, ulnar, and sural nerves. Bilateral values were included when available. Baseline-to-peak amplitudes were measured for compound muscle action potentials. Concomitant diabetes mellitus was defined by hemoglobin A1c values ≥6.5% (national glycohemoglobin standardization program) or the use of glucose-lowering treatment. Regarding treatment response, a good response was defined as an improvement ≥1 point in the Inflammatory Neuropathy Cause and Treatment (INCAT) disability score. A partial response was defined as an improvement of neurologic symptoms, examined by treating neurologists, without achieving a 1-point improvement in the INCAT score.
ELISA
Recombinant human Caspr1 extracellular domain (rhCaspr1; 2418-CR, amino acids 20–1280, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) was maintained at 4°C overnight in 96-well plates (9018, Corning, Corning, NY), with 0.1 μg of protein in 100 μL/well of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). We used identical recombinant protein as in previous research.
18,19,23
The plates were washed with 0.1% Tween 20 in PBS and blocked with 5% skim milk in PBS for 1 hour at room temperature (RT). Serum samples were mixed with 2% skim milk in PBS (1:200 dilution). After washing, the plates were incubated with 100 μL/well of mixed samples for 1 hour at RT. They were then washed and incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated goat anti-human IgG diluted 1:40,000 (ab98624, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) or mouse anti-human IgG1 (A-10648, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), anti-human IgG2 (MH1722, Invitrogen), anti-human IgG3 (05-3620, Invitrogen), and anti-human IgG4 (A-10654, Invitrogen) diluted 1:500 in 2% skim milk in PBS for 1 hour at RT. After washing, 100 μL of tetramethyl benzidine-substrate solution (05298-80, Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) was added to each well and incubated for 10 minutes. Finally, 100 μL of 1 M sulfuric acid was added to each well, and the optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a multi-microplate reader (MTP-800AFC, Corona Electric, Ibaraki, Japan). Each sample was analyzed in duplicate, and the corrected OD was calculated by subtracting the mean OD value of PBS-coated wells from that of rhCaspr1-coated wells to exclude nonspecific background signals. Positive results were defined as corrected OD values >mean + 5 SD of healthy controls. Accordingly, the corrected OD values were termed anti-Caspr1 IgG or IgG subclass titers. Titers of all positive samples were determined by serum dilution from 1:200 to 1:6,400.
Immunohistochemistry
Teased fibers of murine sciatic nerves were prepared as previously described.
6
They were permeabilized with 2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 minutes, blocked in 10% normal goat serum and 1% Triton-X100 in PBS for 1 hour, and then incubated overnight at 4°C with rabbit polyclonal anti-Caspr1 IgG (ab34151, Abcam; 1:500 dilution), which recognizes its intracellular epitope (amino acids 1350 to C-terminal), and serum samples from patients diluted 1:20 in blocking solution. After washing, the fibers were incubated for 1 hour with Alexa 488–labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG and Alexa 647–labeled goat anti-human IgG (Invitrogen) diluted 1:500 in blocking solution. They were then washed, mounted with Fluoromount (Diagnostic BioSystems, Pleasanton, CA), and examined using confocal microscopy (A1, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Staining patterns that were limited to the paranodes with the colocalization of a commercial paranodal marker were defined as positive.
5,8,14
Western Blotting
Each protein sample (per lane: 500 ng of rhCaspr1; R&D Systems) was mixed with reducing or nonreducing sample buffer (09499-14 or 09500-64, respectively, Nacalai Tesque) and heated at 95°C for 3 minutes. Samples were loaded into polyacrylamide gradient gels (4561086, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA) and electrophoresed in Tris-glycine-sodium dodecyl sulfate running buffer. Proteins were then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, Burlington, MA). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS-T) and cut into strips. Each strip was separately incubated overnight at 4°C with serum samples from patients diluted 1:100 or mouse monoclonal anti-Caspr1 IgG (sc-374489, Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX) diluted 1:20,000 in TBS-T with 2% skim milk. Membranes were washed with TBS-T and incubated with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (Abcam) diluted 1:20,000 or horse anti-mouse IgG (PI-2000, Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA) diluted 1:40,000 in TBS-T for 1 hour at RT. After washing, signals were detected using ECL Prime detection reagents (RPN2232, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA) and a ChemiDoc Touch imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Protein levels were normalized using an HRP-conjugated anti-His-tag monoclonal IgG (D291-7, MBL, Tokyo, Japan) diluted 1:5,000 in TBS-T with 2% skim milk.
Immunoadsorption Assay
Serum samples from patients with anti-Caspr1 IgG were diluted 1:20 in blocking solution and preadsorbed with rhCaspr1 (5 μg/mL) for 1 hour at 4°C. The postreaction immunohistochemical protocols were performed as described in the
Immunohistochemistry
section.
Neurofilament Light Chain Measurements
Serum neurofilament light chain (NfL) was measured using a Simoa NF-light V2 advantage kit (104073, Quanterix, Billerica, MA) on a Simoa HD-X analyzer. Serum samples were stored at −80°C until used. They were diluted at 1:4 with sample diluents. Serum NfL levels were analyzed in duplicate following the manufacturer's instructions and standard procedures.
Sural Nerve Biopsy
Pathologic studies of sural nerve biopsy specimens from patients with anti-Caspr1 IgG were performed as previously described.
13
Specimens were fixed with glutaraldehyde and embedded in epoxy resin. Semithin sections were stained with toluidine blue and observed using light microscopy. The degree of pathologic findings was graded into 4 levels: none, mild, moderate, and severe. A portion of each glutaraldehyde-fixed sample was used for a teased fiber study and assessed using previous criteria.
13
The electron microscopic study was performed as previously described.
13
Statistical Analysis
The Mann-Whitney
U
test was used to evaluate median differences between the 2 groups. Categorical data were analyzed using the Fisher exact test. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient was used to assess relationships between continuous variables. Differences in serum NfL levels between subgroups and their correlation with the INCAT disability score were investigated using age at sampling as a candidate covariate. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare differences in paired values at baseline and follow-up.
p
Values <0.05 were considered significant. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP 16.0.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).
Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents
The study was approved by the ethics committee of Kyushu University Hospital (22312-03). Written informed consent was obtained from participants, and an opt-out recruitment method was adopted.
Data Availability
The data sets are not publicly available based on the current consent status of the participants. However, data will be available on reasonable request and with permission of the corresponding author following appropriate procedures.
Results
Anti-Caspr1 IgG Detection by ELISA
We screened 559 serum samples from patients with CIDP for anti-Caspr1 IgG by ELISA (
Figure 1
). Demographic data of patients with CIDP are summarized in eTable 1. Twenty-four patients were seropositive for autoantibodies; none of the healthy (n = 32) or disease (n = 79) controls showed reactivity against Caspr1 (
Figure 2A
). IgG subclass analysis revealed that the IgG4 subclass was predominant, with various levels of IgG1 and IgG2 subclasses in 18 of the 24 patients (
Figure 2B
). Anti-CNTN1 IgG was also screened using live CBA, and 23 cases were identified (
Figure 1
), of which the main subclass was IgG4 in 21 cases and non-IgG4 in 2 cases. One case (patient 17) with weakly positive non-IgG4 anti-CNTN1 IgG staining was also IgG4 anti-Caspr1 IgG positive.
Confirmation of Autoantibody Specificity Against Caspr1
To determine the specificity of the autoantibodies against Caspr1 on native tissue, we performed binding assays using murine teased nerve fibers. Serum samples from all patients with IgG4 anti-Caspr1 IgG except one (patient 18) stained the paranodes and colocalized with a commercial polyclonal anti-Caspr1 IgG, recognizing the intracellular epitope (
Figure 2C
). Moreover, paranodal staining was diminished by preadsorption with rhCaspr1 (eFigure 1). Two patients with non-IgG4 anti-Caspr1 IgG (patients 8 and 13) showed weakly paranodal staining.
To achieve further autoantibody specificity, western blotting using rhCaspr1 was performed under reducing and nonreducing conditions. The band indicating rhCaspr1 was observed under reducing conditions in 4 cases with IgG4 anti-Caspr1 IgG (patients 2, 3, 5, and 20) and 2 with non-IgG4 anti-Caspr1 IgG (patients 8 and 13). Notably, serum samples from all patients with IgG4 anti-Caspr1 IgG except one (patient 15) showed clear reactivity with rhCaspr1 under nonreducing conditions (
Figure 2D
, eFigure 2). Reactivity was stronger under nonreducing conditions than under reducing conditions in all cases with the IgG4 subclass; however, it was comparable under the 2 conditions in non-IgG4 cases (eFigure 2).
The results of immunoassays for anti-Caspr1 IgG are summarized in eTable 2. The 4 samples with the lowest OD values in the ELISA-positive group were considered false positives because they tested negative by both immunohistochemistry and Western blot analysis. Thus, we identified 19 Caspr1 AN—17 with IgG4 subclass predominance—and 1 Caspr1 and CNTN1 AN.
Clinical and Electrophysiologic Features of Caspr1 AN
Table 1
provides the clinical and laboratory features of Caspr1 AN. Of the 17 with IgG4 Caspr1 AN, 13 and 4 cases were categorized into typical and distal phenotypes, respectively. All patients had symmetric 4-limb weakness, hypo/areflexia, and disturbance of deep sensation. Limb weakness was distal or distal-dominant in 12 of the 17 patients (70.6%). Patients with IgG4 Caspr1 AN exhibited an older age at disease onset (67.0 years) and high proportions of gait disturbance (100%), tremor (64.7%), and sensory ataxia (82.4%). Cranial nerve involvement was observed in 5 patients (29.4%), including facial nerve palsy (n = 3), hypogeusia (n = 1), hoarse voice (n = 1), dysarthria (n = 1), and dysphagia (n = 1) (eTable 2). CNS symptoms were observed in 2 patients: one with cerebellar involvement such as slurred speech, nystagmus, and a combination of sensory and cerebellar ataxia and the other with involuntary movements and memory impairments.
28
Seven patients (41.2%) showed autonomic symptoms such as constipation (n = 6), frequent urination (n = 1), dysuria (n = 1), and orthostatic dizziness (n = 1). Four of the 17 patients (23.5%) had diabetes mellitus. In addition, high CSF protein levels (249.4 mg/dL) were observed. No examined patients showed intracerebral lesions strongly suggesting demyelination on brain MRI. Magnetic resonance neurography detected symmetrically mild-to-moderate enlargement of the cervical and lumbosacral roots in 76.9% and 68.8%, respectively, of patients (eFigure 3). In all nerves tested, NCSs showed prolonged F-wave and distal motor latencies and reduced motor nerve conduction velocities (
Table 2
). In addition, patients with IgG4 Caspr1 AN showed slow sensory nerve conduction velocities in all nerves tested.
The 2 patients with non-IgG4 Caspr1 AN were middle-aged women with Sjögren syndrome (
Table 1
). They showed GBS-like acute-onset sensorimotor neuropathy with distal-dominant limb weakness, gait disturbance, and sensory ataxia. Cranial nerve involvement, including facial nerve palsy and hypogeusia, was the initial symptom in both patients. Diabetes mellitus was not concomitant. NCSs revealed apparent conduction delays (
Table 2
).
Pathologic Findings of Sural Nerve Biopsy Specimens
Pathologic studies of biopsied sural nerves from 4 patients (patients 7, 8, 15, and 20) were performed. Using light microscopy, moderate sub-perineurial edema and mild-to-moderate myelinated fiber loss and myelin ovoids, indicating acute axonal degeneration, were observed in all cases (
Figure 3, A and B
). Axonal sprouting, suggesting axonal regeneration, was noted in 1 case (eTable 3). Macrophage-mediated demyelination and onion bulbs were not observed. Inflammatory cell infiltration was absent in 3 cases and mild in 1 case. Although no teased fiber preparations showed segmental demyelination, various degrees of remyelination and axonal degeneration were observed.
For electron microscopy, specimens from 2 patients (patients 8 and 20) were considered suitable for evaluating the morphology of nodes of Ranvier and paranodes. In longitudinal sections from both patients, clear spaces between the terminal myelin loops and axolemma were observed. The extent of axo-glial detachment was severe in patient 20 (IgG4 Caspr1 AN) and mild in patient 8 (non–IgG4 Caspr1 AN) (
Figure 3, C and D
).
Serum NfL Levels
Serum NfL levels were measured in all Caspr1 AN and healthy control samples available, except for patient 14 (
Figure 4A
). Compared with healthy controls, patients with IgG4 Caspr1 AN were older (68.5 vs 46.5 years,
p
= 0.0004) and had higher serum NfL levels (112.6 vs 6.28 pg/mL,
p
< 0.0001) (eTable 4). Two patients with non-IgG4 Caspr1 AN showed high NfL levels of 314.6 pg/mL and 346.8 pg/mL, respectively. Given that NfL concentrations reportedly increase in an age-dependent manner, we compared the serum NfL levels in patients with IgG4 Caspr1 AN with the distribution range in an aged reference population.
29
Except for that of patient 19, whose sample was taken after immunotherapy, the NfL levels of patients with IgG4 Caspr1 AN were much higher than the 95th percentile of the reference data. Furthermore, in a multivariate analysis adjusted for participants' age, the finding of higher serum NfL levels in patients with IgG4 Caspr1 AN than in healthy controls remained close to significance (
p
= 0.0609). In addition, there were higher serum NfL levels in patients with muscle atrophy than in those without (246.0 vs 86.4 pg/mL, respectively,
p
= 0.0361). Higher serum NfL levels correlated with higher INCAT disability scores (
r
= 0.6278,
p
= 0.0092), and this correlation remained (
p
= 0.0143) after correcting for age at sampling. No correlation was observed between serum NfL and CSF protein levels (
r
= 0.4135,
p
= 0.1113).
Treatment Response
Patients with IgG4 Caspr1 AN had moderate severity in the INCAT disability score with a median of 5 (interquartile range [IQR] 3–7, n = 16) at baseline. All patients received immunotherapies. Initial treatment was IVIg (n = 14) or IV methylprednisolone (n = 3). Of 17 patients, 1 patient with a good response to IVIg continued maintenance therapy with IVIg alone. The remaining 16 patients (94.1%) received combined immunotherapy because of the insufficient therapeutic effect of the initial treatment (
Table 3
). Of note, the response rate to IVIg was low in patients with IgG4 Caspr1 AN (31.3%). Of the 5 responders to IVIg, 2 showed a good response and the others had a partial response. The treatment response to daily corticosteroids and plasmapheresis was 90.0% and 50.0%, respectively. Rituximab was used for 3 patients; 2 showed a good response.
Data about the clinical course of disease after immunotherapy over a 6-month follow-up were obtained from 10 patients with IgG4 Caspr1 AN. The median follow-up period was 16 (IQR 7.0–38.5) months. Two patients experienced a relapse once—patient 12 relapsed 3 years after the initial treatment when oral prednisolone was tapered to 5 mg/d, and patient 18, with clinical improvement after initial treatment with pulsed corticosteroids and IVIg, relapsed 7 months later. One patient (patient 16) achieved remission with corticosteroids after not responding well to IVIg, and this state was maintained for more than 2 years after the end of treatment. Six patients (patients 3, 5, 9, 10, 15, and 19) continued to improve under successful treatment. One patient (patient 7) showed severe disease progression from an INCAT score of 5 at baseline to 9 at last follow-up. Other than the 10 patients with more than 6-month clinical course information after immunotherapy, there was 1 refractory case (patient 6) who refused further treatment and nutritional support and progressed to death 4 months after the initial treatment.
Regarding 2 with non-IgG4 Caspr1 AN presenting with acute-onset neuropathy, 1 IVIg-refractory case had a good response to combined therapies with plasmapheresis and corticosteroids. Even after tapering off corticosteroids, the patient maintained remission. Another case receiving a tapering regimen of oral prednisolone after IVIg relapsed at the timing of oral prednisolone 8 mg/d. IVIg maintenance therapy was added on to corticosteroids, with no re-aggravation of clinical symptoms.
Longitudinal Change of Clinical Status, Antibody Titers, and Serum NfL Levels
In 7 patients with serum samples available at 2 time points, including 2 with non-IgG4 Caspr1 AN, the median period from baseline to follow-up was 19 (IQR 6–52) months. All patients showed clinical improvements in INCAT scores and decreased anti-Caspr1 IgG titers (
p
= 0.0156) (
Figure 4B
). Serum NfL levels were also decreased at the follow-up after treatment (
p
= 0.0313) (
Figure 4B
). Detailed treatment information for each patient is listed in eTable 2. Titers returned to normal in 5 of the 7 patients: 3 with IgG4 Caspr1 AN and 2 with non-IgG4 Caspr1 AN (eFigure 4). When patients with both pretreatment and post-treatment serum samples were analyzed, their INCAT scores and serum NfL levels decreased in a similar manner (
Figure 4C
).
Discussion
In this study, we elucidated the comprehensive clinical, laboratory, electrophysiologic, and pathologic characteristics of IgG4 Caspr1 AN. The main findings are as follows: First, IgG4 Caspr1 AN was characterized by an older age at disease onset; distal-dominant limb weakness; gait disturbance with sensory ataxia and tremor; highly elevated CSF protein levels; and a poor response to IVIg, consistent with ANs targeting NF155 or CNTN1.
5-12
Second, NCSs demonstrated severe conduction delays including prolonged F-wave and distal motor latencies and reduced sensorimotor conduction velocities. All patients with Caspr1 AN fulfilled the revised motor and sensory nerve conduction criteria in the EAN/PNS guidelines.
4
Third, axo-glial detachment and an absence of macrophage-mediated demyelination were the pathologic features of sural nerves. Finally, serum NfL levels correlated with INCAT disability scores across patients, and antibody titers and serum NfL levels were associated with clinical severity in individual cases.
Caspr1 AN is considered extremely rare. In Europe, the frequency of anti-Caspr1 IgG has been reported as between 0.2% (2/1,500, CBA) and 2.9% (1/35, CBA) in CIDP subgroups.
14-17
This wide distribution may be caused by several factors including differences in inclusion criteria, races, and autoantibody identification methods. In this study, the positivity rate was 2.7% (19/716), suggesting that Caspr1 AN has a similar incidence to CNTN1 AN in Japan. It is important to exhaustively examine paranodal autoantibodies, including anti-Caspr1 IgG, irrespective of populations.
To date, the clinical profile of Caspr1 AN differs over reports, likely because of relatively small case series. Although some patients reportedly have cranial nerve involvement and severe neuropathic pain,
14,15
these symptoms were not confirmed as specific in our cohort. Our study provides further evidence that IgG4 anti-Caspr1 IgG presents with a conventional phenotype of ANs. One of the major clinical symptoms, sensory ataxia, may be caused by antibody binding to dorsal root ganglia neurons.
19
Among 20 reported cases with IgG4 anti-Caspr1 or anti-Caspr1/CNTN1 complex IgG, the median age at onset was 51.0 (IQR 46.3–57.0) years.
14,15,17,19,22,24,25
This older age at disease onset differs from that of NF155 AN, which exhibits a younger age at disease onset.
6,7,11,12,22
Autoantibodies against CNTN1, which form a cis-complex with Caspr1 on the axolemma, also frequently affect older adults
10-12
; however, CNTN1 AN has a unique comorbidity with membranous nephropathy because CNTN1 is expressed on glomeruli.
27,30
Our study elucidated the electrophysiologic features of IgG4 Caspr1 AN, highlighting marked proximal and distal conduction delays. Reviewing the literature, all 17 reported cases with detailed NCS findings have shown signs of demyelination fulfilling the EFNS/PNS electrodiagnostic criteria for definite CIDP with varying degrees of secondary axonal degeneration, identified as decreased amplitudes on NCSs or spontaneous activities suggesting acute denervation on EMG.
14,15,17,19,24
Regarding treatment, IgG4 Caspr1 AN was refractory to IVIg and required intensive immunotherapy. This poor response to IVIg may be because IgG4 cannot bind to Fc receptors or activate complement.
31
However, some patients with predominantly IgG4 subclass showed a partial response to IVIg, which may be because coexisting IgG1–3 autoantibodies might affect the response to IVIg through complement activation.
32
A good response to rituximab was reported in several case series.
14,19,24
In Japan, rituximab is not covered by medical insurance systems, resulting in its limited use in our cohort. A notable finding in our study was that some patients achieved remission with corticosteroids, suggesting that corticosteroids may be a good therapeutic option—particularly in situations in which rituximab is not approved.
Pathologic studies of peripheral nerves are lacking; light microscopy findings have been previously reported in just 7 cases with anti-Caspr1 and anti-Caspr1/CNTN1 complex IgG, with sub-perineurial edema, loss of myelinated fibers, rare inflammatory cells, and no onion bulbs.
14,19,21,25
Axonal degeneration was observed in 4 of these 7 cases. Our study replicated the axonal degeneration findings with electrophysiologic changes of demyelination and no signs of macrophage-mediated demyelination. Furthermore, the ultrastructural analysis of paranodes in longitudinal sections under electron microscopy has been reported in only 2 previous cases—one with IgG4 and another with unidentified IgG subclass—showing axo-glial detachment.
21,25
In our study, axo-glial detachment was more severe in the case with IgG4 compared with the case with non-IgG4. This finding suggests that the pathogenicity of IgG4 autoantibodies may occur through blocking protein-protein interactions. Similarly, transverse bands between paranodal loops and the axolemma are absent in Caspr1-knockout mice, which alters saltatory conduction through the inappropriate distribution of ion channels and paranodal proteins.
33
The pathogenicity of IgG4 anti-Caspr1 IgG has been suggested from only 1 study in which purified IgG4 from 1 patient disrupted the aggregation between CNTN1/Caspr1-expressing and NF155-expressing cell lines and penetrated the paranodes of murine sciatic nerve fibers after intraneural injections.
17
In our study, serum IgG from most cases with IgG4 anti-Caspr1 IgG targeted nonreduced Caspr1 protein in Western blot analysis, although 4 cases also showed weak reactivity against reduced Caspr1 protein. This finding implies that the autoantibodies might recognize various epitopes, and that most of these polyclonal antibodies mainly target conformational epitopes. Conversely, non-IgG4 anti-Caspr1 IgG may target linear epitopes because the 2 conditions have the same reactivity. Alternatively, reactivity might be associated with comorbid autoimmune diseases that produce various autoantibodies, such as Sjögren syndrome. The pathogenicity of non-IgG4 anti-Caspr1 IgG remains unknown. An IgG subclass switch from non-IgG4 to IgG4 is reportedly observed in the chronic phase of AN.
18
In our cases, we were unable to evaluate switching status because of the negative conversion of anti-Caspr1 IgG in follow-up serum samples. Two patients with non-IgG4 Caspr1 AN in our cohort were middle-aged women who shared an acute-onset neuropathy presenting with cranial nerve involvement, consistent with previous literature.
18
Thus, clinical phenotypes may differ between IgG4 and non-IgG4 subclasses, although larger studies are required. Further studies are also needed for epitope identification and its relationship with phenotype.
Our study has several limitations. First, CBA—a recommended screening test for paranodal autoantibodies in the EAN/PNS guidelines—was not performed.
4
However, ELISA is reportedly more sensitive than CBA for detecting anti-Caspr1 IgG.
19,26
Our in-house ELISA had high specificity, and positive samples were confirmed at multiple dilutions. Moreover, our results were confirmed by immunohistochemistry or Western blot to eliminate false positives and improve specificity. However, multiple assays might have a minimal effect on accuracy
26
; therefore, antibody testing by international reference laboratories would provide further validation of our assay. Second, the clinical profile of CIDP in the antibody-testing patient cohort differed slightly from that of CIDP in the general Japanese population in the latest nationwide survey, with relatively larger proportions of distal type and elevated CSF protein levels.
34
However, patient data may not reflect the characteristics of Caspr1 AN across Japan. The positivity rate (16/78, 21%) for NF155 AN at Kyushu University Hospital was higher than in previous reports
5,11,12
because patients with suspected ANs were referred to Kyushu University Hospital with experts in this field for further investigation and treatment. Third, NCS procedures and MRI sequences were not standardized across hospitals. In addition, spinal nerve root hypertrophy on magnetic resonance neurography was assessed qualitatively because of a lack of consensus about cutoff values.
4
Finally, this was a retrospective study; we acquired data using a questionnaire and medical records, and patients received different immunotherapies at different time points. We were, therefore, unable to provide clear evidence regarding the efficacy of individual immunotherapies. Moreover, differences in clinical and electrophysiologic severity, treatment response, and long-term prognosis between each AN are beyond the scope of this study.
Nevertheless, this large cohort study provides valuable data about the clinical, electrophysiologic, and pathologic features of Caspr1 AN. IgG4 Caspr1 AN presents with a conventional phenotype of ANs, with an older age at disease onset. Diagnostic workups of paranodal autoantibodies should include anti-Caspr1 IgG and IgG subclasses, which can guide optimal treatments. Antibody titers and serum NfL levels may be useful biomarkers for monitoring disease activity and treatment response.
